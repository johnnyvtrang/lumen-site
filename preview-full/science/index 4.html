Science behind the shine.

I'm a scientist by training, and every step of a Lumen detail has a reason you can measure. Here's what pH, iron, hard water, UV and salt actually do to your car, and what I do about each.

pH 7

01 · pH

Neutral by design.

pH measures how acidic or alkaline a liquid is, and the scale is logarithmic: each step is ten times stronger than the last. Strong alkaline cleaners cut road grime fast, but left to dwell they can also strip waxes and light protection and stain sensitive trim. Strong acids dissolve minerals, but they can etch.

So the wash itself is pH-neutral. A neutral shampoo lifts dirt with surfactants and lubrication rather than chemical force, which means it cleans without stripping the ceramic soap, ceramic spray or coating already on your paint. Stronger chemistry is used only where it's needed (wheels, bugs and tar, mineral spots), at the dwell time on the label, and rinsed off.

What it means for you: the protection you paid for stays on the car between visits. How each level is washed.

Fe

02 · Iron

Iron, made visible.

Every time you brake, the pads and rotors shed tiny, hot particles of iron. They land on your wheels and paint, embed in the clear coat and start to rust, which is why paint can feel rough even right after a wash. Cars parked near rail lines collect the same thing as "rail dust".

An iron remover reacts with those particles and turns them into a soluble compound you can rinse away. The reaction is colored: the product turns deep purple as it works, so you can literally watch the contamination dissolve. It's done before any clay or polishing, so the particles aren't dragged across the paint.

Where you get it: iron removal on paint and wheels is part of Clean & Protect and above. Compare the levels.

03 · Hard water

Salt Lake hard water.

Hard water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium. When a drop dries, the water leaves but the minerals stay, as a white ring. Much of the water along the Wasatch Front is hard, and sprinklers, especially those running on untreated secondary irrigation water, are the most common source of spots on cars.

Fresh spots sit on top of the surface and come off chemically with a mild mineral remover. Left to bake in the sun and re-wet again and again, they concentrate and can etch into the clear coat or glass. At that point the ring stays even after cleaning, and only polishing removes it. I'll show you a test spot and quote the polish before going further.

What to do: water spot removal from $45; a ceramic glass or paint coating makes new spots easier to wipe off. Glass coatings and polishing.

UV

04 · Ultraviolet

Why headlights yellow.

Modern headlight lenses are polycarbonate: tough, light, and vulnerable to sunlight. From the factory they carry a thin hard coat with UV absorbers. Years of UV and heat break that coat down, then start to oxidize the plastic underneath. The result is haze, then yellowing, then a lens that scatters light instead of projecting it.

Altitude makes it worse. UV exposure rises with elevation (the World Health Organization puts it at roughly 10% per 1,000 metres), and the valley floor sits around 1,300 metres. Restoration removes the failed, oxidized layer by sanding and machine polishing, which also removes whatever UV protection was left. That's why a new UV coating isn't optional: without it, a freshly polished lens yellows again quickly. It's also why store kits often don't last.

How I do it: three polishing stages, a ceramic UV coating, and a 1-year clarity warranty. Headlight restoration.

Cl−

05 · Road salt

What winter roads leave behind.

Utah roads are treated with salt and, in places, magnesium chloride. Chloride speeds up corrosion of steel whenever moisture is present, and magnesium chloride is hygroscopic: it pulls water out of the air, so it stays damp on the metal longer. It collects on frame rails, brake lines and suspension, exactly where a normal wash never reaches.

A degreasing undercarriage wash lifts the salt and grime off and leaves some anti-corrosion protection behind. After Moab or the dunes, the same wash clears packed mud before it dries hard and traps moisture.

When to book: after storms and at the end of salt season. Winter Salt Flush from $45. Utah extras.

SiO2

06 · Ceramic

Soap, spray, coating: three different things.

"Ceramic" products are built on silica-type chemistry that bonds to the surface and makes it hydrophobic, so water beads and dirt has less to grip. The difference between them is how much is laid down and how long it lasts. A ceramic soap (Gtechniq, in Clean & Protect) leaves a slick layer its maker rates for up to 3 months. A ceramic spray (Adam's graphene ceramic spray, in Clean, Restore & Protect) is hand-applied panel by panel and rated by its maker for 12–18 months.

A ceramic coating is a different product: a much harder, multi-year layer. Because it locks in whatever is underneath, the paint is always polished first. The durability figures here are the manufacturers' ratings; how long protection lasts on your car depends on how it's driven, washed and stored.

Which one is right: compare protection options.

µm

07 · Clear coat

Clear coat is finite.

The clear coat that makes paint glossy is thinner than a sheet of paper, measured in microns. Swirls and light scratches live in that layer, and polishing removes them by levelling a tiny amount of it. Polish too often, or too aggressively, and there's nothing left to protect the color underneath.

So correction starts with paint-depth readings, then a test spot, and takes only as much as the finish needs. You get 50/50 photos, half corrected and half not, so you can see the difference rather than take my word for it.
