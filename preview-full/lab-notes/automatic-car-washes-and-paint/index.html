Lab Notes · Automatic car washes

Why frequent automatic washes wear paint down.

By Johnny Trang, founder · November 2026 · 9 min read

A friction wash drags whatever grit is in its brushes or cloth across the clear coat, leaving shallow marks that add up wash after wash and dull the gloss. A touchless wash avoids that contact by using stronger acid and alkaline chemistry instead. That's easier on the paint than brushes, but it's harder on wax, toppers and coatings. Neither one is a disaster now and then. A wash membership used every week turns small effects into a visible pattern.

This is a common, reasonable habit. Automatic washes are fast, they get salt and dust off in winter, and a membership makes them cheap enough to use often. Nobody notices one wash's worth of marks. This note explains what each part of the wash does to paint, so you can decide how often it's worth it for your car.

Brushes, cloth and grit: where swirls come from

Paint researchers sort surface damage into two kinds. A fracture scratch breaks the clear coat; it comes from keys, branches and other hard objects. A mar is a shallow groove, a few microns wide and deep, that deforms the clear coat without breaking it. Automotive coating researchers name automatic car-wash brushes, along with home washing, as the typical cause of mars.1 Researchers at the National Institute of Standards and Technology say these shallow marks most often come from car washing.2

What does the cutting is the dirt, not the brush alone. The standard lab test for car-wash resistance, ISO 20566, simulates a wash with a rotating brush and water carrying fine silica powder as synthetic dirt, because that is what a real wash brush carries across a car.3 The same coating review notes that wiping damage happens at a much higher rate when the wiping material is embedded with a contaminant such as road grit.1 A brush or cloth strip that has just washed a muddy truck reaches your car next.

The brush itself changes with use. The ISO standard warns that the test grows more severe as the brush ages, so results from brushes with different hours on them can't be compared.3 That's a lab note, not a verdict on any particular wash, but it shows the brush material matters.

What frequency adds up to

One mar is almost invisible. The coating review describes what happens over time: as paint is exposed to continued car washing, the number of mars increases, and so does their visibility. Once there are enough of them, they're easy to see on a bright, sunny day when you look along the paint at a shallow angle.1 In large numbers they lower the overall gloss of the clear coat.1

The arithmetic of a membership is simple. Once a week is 52 trips through the brushes in a year, and about 260 over five years. Each one adds its share of marks, and dark paint shows them first, because swirls scatter light and that scatter shows against a dark, mirror-like reflection.

Getting the gloss back means removing them, and that means removing clear coat. Polishing levels the paint down to the floor of each groove, and clear coat doesn't grow back. Most factory clear coat is about 35–50 µm thick, and one gauge maker reports that most carmakers advise removing no more than about 8 µm of it.4 That's why we measure before we polish; see Paint depth gauging.

Pre-soaks: strong chemistry, in both kinds of wash

pH measures how acidic or alkaline a liquid is: 7 is neutral, lower is acidic, higher is alkaline. Automatic washes use both ends of the scale on purpose. A car-wash trade article explains that acidic detergents target inorganic soils (clay, minerals, brake dust, salts) and alkaline detergents target organic soils (oils, road film, bugs), and that most express tunnels use a two-step process: a high-pH cleaner first, then a low-pH one, with the brushes helping in between.5

Touchless washes flip that order, low pH first and then high pH, because there's no friction to help.5 Another trade article on touch-free chemistry says these systems need stronger acids and alkalis, and stronger surfactants, to make up for the lack of contact.6 A car-wash chemical supplier puts it plainly: touchless washes typically need stronger formulations because chemicals are the only thing cleaning the car.7

Those products are built to be safe on paint when they're diluted and timed correctly; the trade article gives an ideal dwell of 30 to 45 seconds per pass.6 But you can't see the dilution, the dwell or whether it dried on a hot panel, and strong cleaners wear down wax, ceramic sealants and coating toppers faster than a pH-neutral wash does. That's why coating makers write it into their care rules. One coating maker's care guidance lists automatic car washes and harsh or acidic soaps as things to avoid, and its own top-layer coating page says hand washing is recommended and, if an automatic wash is used, touchless is preferred over brushes.8,9

Touchless, fairly

Touchless washes trade friction for chemistry. Nothing drags grit across the paint, so they don't cause brush marring. In return, the chemistry is stronger, and a film of road grime that only friction would have lifted can stay on the paint. For salt in a Utah winter, when a hand wash isn't possible, an occasional touchless wash is a reasonable compromise. As a weekly habit on a coated car, it wears the topper and coating faster than a pH-neutral hand wash.

Recycled water and spotting

Many washes reclaim and reuse their water. One operator writing in a trade magazine describes reusing up to 80% of his wash water, with fresh reverse-osmosis (RO) water used only for the spot-free final rinse, and advises against putting reclaim water through the rinse arches because of the sediment and impurities it carries.10 A wash-chemistry supplier notes that most washes use fresh water for the final or spot-free rinse, and that some reuse over 90% of their water.11

So "recycled water" doesn't mean your car is rinsed in dirty water at the end; a well-run wash filters it and keeps it for the early stages. Spotting comes from what's dissolved in the last water left on the car. The same operator calls RO essential in arid climates, where water spotting and dry heat cause problems, particularly on darker vehicles.10 Salt Lake water carries a lot of dissolved minerals, so any drop that dries before it's rinsed with clean water can leave a ring. More on that in Hard-water spots.

Dryers

The big blowers at the exit don't touch the paint. An equipment maker points out that most of them aren't heated dryers at all, but blowers designed to strip residual water off an already-clean car.12 Their limit is what they miss. A trade article on drying describes water held in mirrors and cowlings that streams out after the blowers and dries in streaks, and notes that with an RO final rinse, a missed drop evaporates without leaving a spot.13 Where a wash finishes with towels, a chemical supplier notes that the less water is left on the car, the less towel work and wipe-induced swirl there is.14

What we tell owners

  • The care card with every coating says pH-neutral soap only, no automatic washes, and touchless only if one is unavoidable in winter. It also says dry with a blower or a plush towel. The reasons are the ones above.
  • If you use a membership, that's your call to make. Touchless is the gentler of the two automatic kinds for paint, and dark paint will show brush marks first.
  • For coated, PPF, wrapped or matte cars, our Coated Care Wash is a finish-safe hand wash with a topper matched to the finish. It's described on Wrap, matte & PPF care. A careful pH-neutral hand wash at home does the same job; here's how.
  • If the paint already looks hazy or swirled, it isn't necessarily failing. Marring sits in the top of the clear coat, and a measured correction can often bring most of the gloss back. We gauge every panel first and test a small spot before deciding anything.

Questions

Will one automatic wash ruin my paint?

No. A single wash leaves marks too small to see. The concern is frequency: as paint keeps going through car washes, the marks multiply and become visible in bright sun, and the gloss drops.

Is a touchless wash safe?

It avoids brush marring, because nothing touches the paint. It uses stronger acid and alkaline chemistry instead, which wears wax, toppers and coatings faster. An occasional touchless wash in winter is a reasonable compromise; as a weekly habit, a pH-neutral hand wash is gentler.

My paint looks dull and swirled. Does it need a repaint?

Often not. Wash marring sits in the top few microns of the clear coat. If a gauge shows healthy paint depth and a test spot responds, correction can bring much of the gloss back. A gauge reading and a test spot answer that; a guess doesn't.
