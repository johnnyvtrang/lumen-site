Lab Notes · Hard water

Hard-water spots in Utah, and how they come off.

By Johnny Trang, owner · Updated November 2026 · 13 min read

Hard-water spots are the calcium, magnesium and silica left behind when Wasatch Front tap or sprinkler water dries on a car, and most of them come off with a mild acid if you catch them early. Spots that have baked on need a stronger mineral remover and clay, and spots that have etched into the clear coat or glass only come out by polishing, which removes a little of the surface for good. The cheapest fix is prevention: dry the car, keep sprinklers off it, and rinse with deionized water.

How hard is the water here?

Water hardness is the amount of dissolved calcium and magnesium in water. It is usually reported as milligrams per liter (mg/L) of calcium carbonate, and mg/L is the same as parts per million (ppm). The U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) calls 0 to 60 mg/L soft, 61 to 120 mg/L moderately hard, 121 to 180 mg/L hard, and anything over 180 mg/L very hard.1

By that scale, most of the Wasatch Front is hard or very hard. Salt Lake City's 2025 water quality report lists total hardness by source:2

  • Big Cottonwood treatment plant: 121 mg/L
  • Parleys treatment plant: 174 mg/L
  • City Creek treatment plant: 198 mg/L
  • Water bought from Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District: about 189 mg/L
  • The city's own wells: 157 to 487 mg/L

West Valley City is my home base. Much of it is served by the Granger-Hunter Improvement District, which blends its own wells with treated water from Jordan Valley. Its 2026 report puts average hardness at about 176 mg/L, or about 10 grains per gallon.3

Grains per gallon (gpg) is the older unit you see on water softeners. One grain is 64.8 mg and one U.S. gallon is 3.785 liters, so 1 gpg is about 17.1 mg/L. Salt Lake City's report does the same math: 121 mg/L is 7.1 gpg, and its hardest well water, 487 mg/L, is 28.6 gpg.2

Hardness is only part of what is in the water. The same report lists total dissolved solids (TDS), everything dissolved in it, from 205 mg/L to 804 mg/L depending on the source.2 Put simply, every liter of that water that dries on a car leaves roughly 0.2 to 0.8 grams of solids behind. That is what you are looking at when a dark hood turns speckled white.

What is actually in a spot

Calcium and magnesium carbonates. Most of the hardness here travels as calcium and magnesium paired with bicarbonate. Salt Lake City reports 94 to 258 mg/L of bicarbonate across its sources.2 Hardness that is matched by bicarbonate and carbonate is called carbonate hardness.4 As a drop warms and dries, carbon dioxide leaves the water and the dissolved calcium falls out as solid calcium carbonate, the same white scale that builds up in a water heater:1

Ca2+ + 2 HCO3− → CaCO3 (solid) + CO2 + H2O

Magnesium does the same thing to a lesser degree. Those carbonates are the part an acid can dissolve.

Silica. Silica (silicon dioxide) dissolves into water from rock and soil, commonly at less than 30 mg/L, and the USGS notes that it forms hard scale in pipes and boilers.4 Salt Lake City's report doesn't list silica, so I can't give you a local number. What matters for your car is that silica is not a carbonate. The acid reaction below doesn't break it down, which is one reason some spots survive a mineral remover.

Everything else. Sodium and chloride, also listed in the report, make up part of the rest of the dissolved solids, and they dry along with the hardness minerals.2

Why a spot is a ring. A drying drop usually stays pinned at its edge. Water evaporating from that edge is replaced by water flowing out from the middle, and the flow carries dissolved and suspended material to the rim. Physicists at the University of Chicago showed this in 1997 to explain coffee rings.5 It is why a water spot is usually a crisp white outline with a cleaner center.

The three stages

A spot starts as loose minerals on top of the surface and can end as a pit in it. Where it sits on that path decides how it comes off.

  1. Stage 1: surface deposit. Fresh minerals sitting on the surface. They haven't bonded and often wipe off with water or a quick detailer.
  2. Stage 2: bonded spot. Minerals baked on by sun and repeated wetting. They survive a wash and a gentle wipe, but the surface under them is still intact.
  3. Stage 3: etched spot. The surface itself has been eaten into. Under a light it shows as a dull ring or a shallow crater even after the minerals are gone.

Automotive paint chemists see the same split. In a Ford Motor Company study of environmental etch, which looks like water spotting that won't wash off, damage was rated in three groups: not visible to the owner, visible but repairable by polishing, and repairable only by repainting.7

1 · Surface depositComes off with a quick detailer or water wipeMild acid, rinse, dry, protect
2 · Bonded spotSurvives a wipe; comes off with a mineral removerAcidic mineral remover, then clay, then protect
3 · Etched spotRing still visible under a light after the removerMachine polishing, measured and least aggressive first

Why sprinklers and sun make it worse

Sprinklers wet the same spots over and over. A car parked by a lawn gets misted on the same side, in the same places, several times a week. Many Utah neighborhoods water lawns with secondary water, which the state health department describes as untreated water from reservoirs, rivers, lakes, ponds and canals, meant only for lawns and landscapes.6 Untreated doesn't by itself mean harder, and I haven't found published hardness numbers for local secondary systems. But a spot that dries, re-wets and dries again keeps adding minerals in the same ring.

Repetition is also how etching grows. The Ford study describes etch pits forming as droplets evaporate and their chemistry concentrates, with new droplets settling into the shallow dips left by earlier ones.7 That study was about acid rain, not hard water. The mechanism of mineral-spot etching on clear coat is less well studied, so treat that as a close analogy rather than proof.

Heat speeds everything up. The same researchers ran their tests at 60 °C (140 °F), the commonly accepted surface temperature of a dark car on a summer day in Florida.7 Heat dries drops faster, drives off carbon dioxide so carbonate falls out sooner, and speeds up chemical reactions in general. A common rule of thumb is that a 10 °C rise roughly doubles a reaction's rate, though it doesn't hold for every reaction.8

Glass etches too. Car glass is a silicate glass. Glass chemists describe water attacking it in two stages: first ions at the surface are swapped out, then, once the film on the glass turns alkaline above about pH 9, the silica network itself starts to break down.10 A mineral drop that dries in the same place day after day gives that chemistry many chances. It is why windshields under sprinklers often show rings that no cleaner touches.

How acids dissolve carbonate spots

pH is a measure of how acidic or alkaline something is: 7 is neutral, lower is acidic, higher is alkaline. Salt Lake City's tap water runs from about pH 6.8 to 8.0.2 An acid supplies hydrogen ions (H+), and hydrogen ions react with carbonate and bicarbonate to make carbon dioxide and water:9

CaCO3 + 2 H+ → Ca2+ + CO2 + H2O

The calcium goes back into solution as a dissolved ion and rinses away. Distilled white vinegar works this way through acetic acid. Dedicated mineral removers use stronger or blended acids, and they work faster on bonded spots.

Three things follow from that chemistry:

  • Short dwell, cool panel, never let it dry. An acid that dries on a hot panel concentrates just like a water spot. Acid is also what attacks clear coat in environmental etch.7 So the product goes on a cool panel, sits 30 to 60 seconds, and comes off.
  • Rinse or neutralize every time. Once the spots are gone, the acid's job is done. Clean water or a pH-neutral wipe takes away what's left.
  • Acid doesn't fix etching or silica. It can only dissolve the carbonate on top. A ring that remains after a remover is either a stubborn non-carbonate deposit or a dent in the surface.

Some finishes shouldn't see acid at all, or only the mildest kind after a test spot: paint protection film, vinyl wraps, matte paint, raw, polished or anodized aluminum, older single-stage paint, and chrome-look plastic trim.

The removal ladder, least to most aggressive

The rule is to start at the bottom and climb only as far as the spots force you to. Every step up removes more, and the last steps remove surface that doesn't grow back.

  1. Wash and dry. A pH-neutral wash, then a full dry. You can't judge spots through dirt.
  2. Water or quick detailer wipe. If the spots come off, they were stage 1.
  3. Mild acid. A light mineral remover, or distilled white vinegar cut about 1:1 with distilled water. Mist one small section, wait 30 to 60 seconds, wipe with a clean, folded microfiber, then rinse.
  4. Dedicated acidic mineral remover. Sensitive trim and film edges masked first. Worked gently for 30 to 60 seconds, wiped off fully, and rinsed or neutralized right away. Up to two or three passes on a section.
  5. Clay. A clay bar or mitt with plenty of lubricant lifts the residue the acid loosened and leaves the surface smooth.
  6. Machine polishing. Only for stage 3 rings. A finishing polish first, stepping up one level at a time only if the rings remain.
  7. Refinishing. Etch too deep to polish out safely means wet sanding by a body shop or a repaint, the last group in the Ford rating.7

When polishing is needed, and what it costs the paint

Polishing doesn't fill an etched ring. It levels the surface around it until the ring is gone or shallow enough not to show. That means taking off clear coat, the clear top layer that protects the color and gives the shine.

There isn't much of it. Clear coat usually runs 35 to 50 microns (µm) thick, and most factory paint, all layers together, is 100 to 180 µm. Most carmakers recommend removing no more than about 8 µm of clear coat, and some now specify measuring paint thickness before and after any sanding or buffing.11

So I measure paint depth on each affected panel with a paint depth gauge before any polishing, and I write the readings down. Repainted panels and edges are often thinner, and those get minimal correction or none. On a test section I start with a finishing polish on a soft pad, tape a 50/50 line, and step up to a medium polish and then a compound only if the rings are still there. Whatever is least aggressive and still works is what goes on the rest of the panel. After any compound, a finishing polish removes the haze it leaves.

Sometimes the honest answer is that a ring can be improved but not erased without cutting more clear coat than the panel can spare. When the gauge says stop, I stop and tell you why.

Glass is different. Etched glass is polished wet, at low speed, with a cerium-oxide glass polish on a felt or rayon pad. Heat is the danger. Uneven heat can stress glass, and a windshield in cold weather is the worst case, so the glass is kept wet and touch-checked often. Chasing a deep ring in one spot can also leave a lens-like distortion, which matters most in the driver's line of sight. The goal is glass that is improved and optically clean, not perfect. Deep etching may remain. More on glass on the glass and wheels page.

Deionized water and ion exchange

If minerals in the rinse water cause the spots, the fix is rinse water with almost no minerals. Deionized (DI) water is made by ion exchange. Water passes over small plastic resin beads with charged groups built in. Cation resin swaps positive ions such as calcium, magnesium, iron and sodium for hydrogen ions. Anion resin swaps negative ions such as chloride, sulfate, carbonate and silica for hydroxide ions. The hydrogen and hydroxide then join into plain water.12

Two details matter for cars:

  • Mixed beds give the purest water. Mixing both resins in one tank repeats the exchange many times over and gives the highest quality DI water. Only strong-base anion resin removes silica, which is part of what makes spots stubborn.12
  • Resin runs out. Once every exchange site is used, the tank stops producing DI water until it is regenerated or replaced. Fewer ions means lower electrical conductivity, so a simple conductivity or TDS meter shows when the water is no longer clean.12

A drop of DI water that dries leaves almost nothing behind. That is why my final rinses use DI water. Hard tap water from a spigot will spot dark paint in the sun, even while you are still washing.

Drying technique

Without DI water, drying is the whole game. Water that is removed before it evaporates takes its minerals with it.

  • Wash in shade, on a cool car. Early morning or evening in summer. A hot panel can dry a drop before you reach it.
  • Work in small sections. Wash and rinse one area at a time on hot or windy days, so nothing dries between rinse and towel.
  • Blow, then blot. A blower pushes water out of seams, mirrors and badges, where drips run out later and spot. A plush drying towel picks up what's left with light passes instead of scrubbing.
  • Use a fresh towel. A soaked towel spreads mineral water thin and leaves streaks.
  • Never let it air-dry in the sun. That is how most stage 1 spots start.

How coatings resist minerals, and how they clog

Protection doesn't stop minerals from being in the water. It changes what the water does on the surface. On a protected, slick surface, water beads up and much of it rolls off, so less of it sits and dries. And the minerals that do dry sit on the protection rather than on bare clear coat, which is why they usually come off more easily.

The catch is that the beads that stay still dry. Research on evaporating mineral-laden drops shows that a water-repellent surface alone doesn't reliably prevent deposits. What matters is whether the drop's edge stays pinned while it dries, and that depends on the surface's fine texture.13 In practice, a coated car in the sprinklers still spots. The spots are just easier to remove.

Over months, minerals and road film also build up as a thin layer on top of a coating. The coating is still there, but water stops beading, and it looks worn out. That is a clogged coating. A mineral decon dissolves the layer and often brings the beading back. One coating maker rates its coating to tolerate pH 2 to 11, which is why a mild acid can be used on it.14 The same maker warns that its acidic water spot remover shouldn't go on fresh coatings and may reduce coating performance,14 so coated cars get a coating-safe mineral remover and no strong acids.

The layers differ in how long they last. A ceramic soap is maker-rated up to 3 months. A hand-applied ceramic sealant is maker-rated up to 6 months or 50 washes. A spray coating is maker-rated up to 12 to 18 months. Paint coatings are maker-rated up to 24 months (Essential), 36 months (Signature), and 50 months to five years (Reserve).15 Those are the makers' ratings. Real life depends on how the car is washed, parked and stored, and sprinklers shorten it.

A coating also locks in whatever is under it, etched rings included, so paint is polished before any coating goes on. More on picking one in how to choose a ceramic coating, and on the chemistry in the science of coatings.

How I handle water spots

  1. Wash and dry first, with a pH-neutral wash, so the spots can be judged clean.
  2. Inspect under a light. An LED inspection light or low sun shows where the spots are: paint, glass, trim, wheels, chrome. Acid-sensitive surfaces get flagged.
  3. Test spot. A wipe, then a mineral remover, on one small area. That tells me which stage it is.
  4. Photos and a quote before the whole car. You see the before and after of the test spot and approve the price first. I also tell you in writing that etching can be improved but not always removed, and that polishing can only go as far as the paint thickness allows.
  5. Shade, cool panels, small sections, about 2 by 2 feet on paint and one window at a time on glass, with every acid step rinsed or neutralized.
  6. Depth gauge before any polishing, least aggressive first, and a stop where the paint says stop.
  7. DI water for final rinses, then the protection that comes with the level you booked. Nothing extra is added. If chemical removal isn't enough, I stop and re-quote before polishing, and nothing goes ahead without your OK.

Then the one tip that does the most: park away from sprinklers or turn the heads, and dry the car after every wash. The full chemistry summary is on the science page.

Questions

Is vinegar safe for water spots on my car?

On fresh spots on intact clear coat and glass, distilled white vinegar cut about 1:1 with distilled water works and is gentle. Use it in the shade on a cool panel, leave it 30 to 60 seconds, wipe it off and rinse. Don't let it dry, and keep it off film, wraps, matte paint, raw aluminum and chrome-look plastic unless a test spot says it's fine. If the spots don't move, vinegar won't fix them; they've bonded or etched.

Why do my water spots come back?

Because the source is still there. Most repeat spots come from sprinklers hitting the same side of the car, or from letting rinse water dry in the sun. Moving the car or the sprinkler heads, drying after every wash, and keeping protection on the paint all help. Protection makes spots easier to remove; it doesn't stop minerals from drying on the car.

Can etched water spots be fully removed?

Often, but not always. Polishing levels the surface around a ring, and clear coat is thin, usually 35 to 50 microns. I measure paint depth before polishing and stop when cutting further would be unsafe for the paint. Deep rings in glass can also remain, because over-polishing one spot can distort the view. I'll show you a test section first so you know what to expect.

My car is coated. Why does it still spot?

A coating makes water bead and roll off, but the beads that stay still dry and leave minerals. Over time those minerals build up a film on top of the coating and the beading fades. A coating-safe mineral decon usually brings it back. Strong acids aren't used on coatings.
