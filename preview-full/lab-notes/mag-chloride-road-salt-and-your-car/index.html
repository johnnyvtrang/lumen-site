Lab Notes · Road salt

Mag chloride, road salt and your car.

By Johnny Trang, founder · Updated November 2026 · 13 min read

Utah roads get salt brine before storms and rock salt during them, with salts treated with magnesium and calcium when it gets colder, and every one of them corrodes steel and aluminum because chloride turns a film of water into a strong electrolyte and breaks down the oxide skin that protects bare metal. Magnesium chloride is the stickier problem: it pulls water out of air as dry as about 33% humidity, so it stays wet under the car long after the road dries, and the only real fix is to wash it off underneath, through the season and again in spring.

What Utah actually puts on the road

UDOT, which clears more than 24,000 lane miles, describes its winter toolkit plainly. Before a storm, crews pre-treat with salt brine. During storms they use regular white salt, three types of high-performance salt, de-slicking grit and volcanic cinders. In some places with high ice concerns, fixed spray systems apply potassium acetate, a deicer that isn't a chloride.1

UDOT's page doesn't spell out what is in the high-performance salts. A 2014 report on UDOT's methods described them as salts treated with magnesium and calcium that crews switch to when the temperature drops below 18 °F, because they keep working down to about 5 °F. The same report credited the brine-first approach with saving about 30 percent of the salt UDOT would otherwise use.2

Cities and counties run their own trucks and buy their own materials, so the mix on a residential street can differ from the mix on I-15. One state rule sets the floor. Utah's air-quality rule on road salting requires that any salt applied to roads in Salt Lake, Davis and Utah counties be at least 92% sodium chloride, magnesium chloride, calcium chloride and/or potassium chloride.3 Magnesium chloride deicer is also a local product: it has been made at plants along the Great Salt Lake and shipped to other western states for decades.4

The short version for a car owner: what lands on your underbody in a Utah winter is mostly chloride, as dry crystals, wet brine or slush, whatever the label on the truck.

Why these salts work on ice

Dissolved salt lowers the temperature at which water freezes. Each salt has a limit, called the eutectic point: the concentration that gives the lowest possible freezing temperature. For sodium chloride (NaCl, rock salt) that is about 23% salt by weight, which freezes at about −21 °C (−6 °F). For magnesium chloride (MgCl2) it is 21.6%, which freezes at about −33 °C (−28 °F).5

Those are laboratory limits. On a real road the brine keeps getting diluted by melting snow, so the practical range is much warmer. Federal guidance notes evidence that anti-icing with salt shouldn't be done at or below about −9.5 °C (15 °F) pavement temperature, and that a magnesium chloride brine can be diluted further than a sodium chloride brine before it refreezes.5 That is why agencies reach for magnesium and calcium blends when it gets cold. For a car, that means chloride of one kind or another underneath through the whole season, not just the mildest storms.

Why chloride corrodes steel

Rust is electrochemistry. On a wet steel surface, some spots act as an anode, where iron gives up electrons and dissolves (Fe → Fe2+ + 2e−). Other spots act as a cathode, where dissolved oxygen takes those electrons and becomes water. The film of water joining them works like the salt bridge in a battery, and the dissolved iron is later oxidized by air into rust, hydrated iron(III) oxide.6 No water, no circuit. No oxygen, no cathode.

Salt makes that circuit far more efficient. Pure water carries current poorly; a brine is full of ions, so anodes and cathodes can work across a much larger area. Chloride also does something more targeted. Most steel and many coatings rely on a thin oxide or hydroxide film that slows corrosion to almost nothing. Chloride disrupts that film at local spots, and each spot becomes an active anode surrounded by a large passive cathode. If enough chloride is present, the pit keeps growing instead of healing over.7 That is pitting: small on the surface, deep underneath.

Timing matters as much as chemistry. In laboratory work on steel in deicer brines, steel kept through wet and dry cycles corroded the most, more than steel kept through freeze and thaw.7 A car in a Utah winter lives in that wet and dry cycle: splashed on the drive, dried in the garage or the afternoon sun, re-wetted by the next storm.

Car makers fight this with zinc. Galvanized steel has a thin zinc layer that oxidizes before the steel does, sacrificing itself to protect it.6 That works well, but zinc is consumed in the process. Salt exposure spends that protection faster.

Aluminum isn't immune

Aluminum protects itself with a very thin oxide film, which is why it doesn't rust the way steel does. Chloride is the classic way that film fails. In a 2023 study using depth profiling, chloride was found moving into the oxide film of aluminum soaked in a chloride solution, getting deeper with longer exposure. The film hydrated and slowly dissolved, and pits started at the boundary between metal and film, mostly next to tiny iron- and silicon-rich particles in the metal. Inside a pit the dissolving aluminum makes the liquid more acidic, and more chloride moves in to balance the charge, which keeps the pit going.8

On a car that means alloy wheels, many suspension parts and some body panels. Aluminum corrosion shows up as white, powdery deposits and pits rather than red rust, and it tends to start wherever the factory finish has been chipped or scraped.

Why mag chloride stays wet and clings

Two terms first. A hygroscopic material pulls water vapor out of the air. A deliquescent material pulls in so much that it dissolves in that water and turns into a liquid. Both depend on relative humidity, and each salt has its own threshold.

That threshold can be measured precisely: it is the humidity of the air sitting over a saturated solution of the salt. A National Bureau of Standards compilation gives about 33% relative humidity for magnesium chloride and about 75% for sodium chloride, and both values barely change between freezing and 25 °C (77 °F).9 Federal deicing guidance gives the same picture for rock salt: it doesn't begin absorbing moisture until about 76% humidity.5

Put plainly: dried rock salt on a frame rail stays as crystals until the air gets quite damp. Dried magnesium chloride turns back into a liquid brine whenever the air is above about a third humidity. That liquid is the electrolyte steel needs to corrode.

Colorado tested this directly. The state's study compared the two salts on stainless steel, aluminum, coated car-body sheet, copper wire and mild steel. In the cyclic humid test, "MgCl2 was more corrosive than NaCl to the bare metals tested."10 Two other test methods disagreed, and the authors traced the conflict to moisture. Magnesium chloride brine is more viscous and more attracted to water than sodium chloride brine. It clings to metal, crystallizes when dry and redissolves when damp. Their conclusion: magnesium chloride is more corrosive in humid conditions, and sodium chloride more corrosive under immersion and in dry conditions. Colorado then required its magnesium chloride deicer to be no more corrosive than sodium chloride on aluminum and stainless steel under a standard test.10

A later review put the same idea in one phrase: the hygroscopic chlorides of magnesium and calcium can be more aggressive to exposed metal than sodium chloride "because of the longer time of wetness."7 Corrosion engineers call this time of wetness: the hours a metal surface spends under a film of water. More hours wet means more hours of corrosion.

Where the damage happens on a car

Chloride does its damage wherever brine collects, stays wet and touches bare or thinly protected metal. On most vehicles that is a short list.

Undercarriage and frame
Frame rails, crossmembers, seams and pinch welds. Overlapping metal makes a crevice that holds brine and starves it of oxygen at the bottom, which is the anode and cathode arrangement chloride pitting likes. Closed frame sections drain through small holes that slush can plug.
Brake lines
Thin steel tubing run along the frame. When federal regulators investigated brake-pipe corrosion failures in one family of pickups and SUVs from 1999 to 2003, the pipes were double-wall steel with a hot-dip zinc-aluminum coating and an aluminum paint outer layer. Failures were rare before 8 years of service, then rose in salt states from under 0.1 per thousand vehicles at 7 years to over 1.0 after the 12th year. The agency put it down to the coating wearing out in severe corrosion states, not a manufacturing defect.11 Utah isn't on the regulator's salt-state list for these investigations, but the chemistry doesn't check state lines. Closing that probe, the agency urged owners to have undercarriages washed several times during and after winter, and to have brake lines checked.12
Wheel wells
Tires throw brine straight up into the wells, and slush packs in behind the liners, holding salty water against metal for days.
Rocker panels and lower doors
The lowest painted panels take the most spray. Door bottoms and rockers have drain holes, and when those plug, water and salt sit inside the panel.
Wheels
Alloy wheels are aluminum under paint and clear coat. Curb rash, chips and worn finish near the spokes and lug seats expose metal, and corrosion can then creep under the finish from the edge of the damage.
Paint chips and clear coat
Paint protects steel only where it is intact. A rock chip that reaches metal leaves exposed steel next to coated steel, and two scratches some distance apart can work together as anode and cathode. Corrosion that starts at a chip often spreads as pits hidden under the surrounding paint.6 That is the bubbling you see at the edge of an old chip.

What rinsing does and doesn't do

Road salt is a neutral salt. pH has nothing to do with removing it, and no product chemically cancels it. Water dissolves it and carries it away, and that's the entire mechanism. A rinse works by dilution: every pass of clean water lowers the chloride left in a seam until what remains is too little to matter.

What a rinse doesn't do:

  • It doesn't reach what water can't reach. The inside of closed frame sections, under seam sealer and behind tightly fitted liners all stay as they were. A quick pass leaves a thin brine in every crevice it doesn't flush.
  • It doesn't cut road film. Underneath, salt sits in an oily layer of road grime. Plain water runs over oil, which is why an undercarriage wash uses a degreaser first.
  • It doesn't last. The next drive on treated roads puts brine right back. Washing resets the clock; it doesn't stop it.
  • It doesn't remove rust. Once metal has corroded, washing only slows what happens next.

A rinse can also cause its own problems in the cold. Water in door seals, locks and brake parts can freeze. That's why the Winter Salt Flush is done only on above-freezing days, so nothing freezes on the car or the driveway.

How often to wash in a Utah winter

There is no lab-tested number of days. The useful idea is time of wetness: the less time brine spends on the metal, the less corrosion it can drive. That points to a rhythm built around your driving rather than the calendar.

  1. After storms. Once roads have been treated and you've driven on them, the brine is already underneath. Washing in the days after gets it off before it sits for weeks.
  2. Through the season, scaled to exposure. A daily commute on salted highways or up the canyons collects far more than a car that mostly stays parked. More treated miles, more washes.
  3. On above-freezing days. So the rinse water drains and dries instead of freezing in seams and locks.
  4. At the end of salt season. One last thorough wash underneath, so the winter's buildup doesn't sit there through the warm, humid months.

A note on heated garages. A warm garage melts the slush off the car, which keeps the underside wet, and corrosion, like most chemistry, runs faster when it's warmer.7 The garage isn't the problem; the brine that came home with the car is.

How coatings help, and where they stop

A ceramic coating cures into a thin silica (SiO2) glass layer on the clear coat, and treated surfaces hold onto less grime than untreated ones.13 In winter that does two useful things.

  • Release. Brine film and road grime bond less to a coated surface, so they rinse off with less scrubbing.
  • Chemical resistance. Coatings are rated to tolerate a range of chemistry; one maker lists a pH tolerance of 2 to 11 for its paint coating.14 Road salt is near neutral, so the coating itself isn't what's at risk. What it shields is the clear coat under it, from grime that sits between washes.

The limits matter just as much:

  • A coating protects only what it covers. Paint coatings go on paint, wheels and glass. Frame rails, brake lines and suspension aren't coated by a paint coating, and that's where most salt damage happens.
  • It doesn't stop rock chips. Paint protection film is made for that. Once a chip reaches metal, the coating around it isn't protecting the steel inside it.
  • It doesn't replace washing. It makes washing faster and easier. It still needs washing, and the right care, to reach its rated life.

Durability is the maker's rating, and Utah winters are a hard test of any of them. In the order we offer them: ceramic soap is maker-rated up to 3 months; hand-applied ceramic sealant up to 6 months or 50 washes; spray coating up to 12 to 18 months; and paint coatings up to 24 months for Essential, up to 36 months for Signature, and up to 50 months to five years for Reserve. A coating locks in whatever is under it, so we measure paint depth and polish the paint before any coating goes on. Coatings go on in your garage when you have one; otherwise we use coatings made for outdoor application and plan the day around the weather. More on choosing one is in how to choose a ceramic coating for how you drive.

Washing the undercarriage

The underside is where salt does the most damage and where a normal wash reaches least. That's what the Winter Salt Flush is for. It's a degreasing undercarriage wash, done at your home anywhere we work in the Salt Lake Valley:

  1. Loosen. Slush, loose salt and mud come off first with an undercarriage wand on the pressure washer.
  2. Degrease. A degreaser goes on from the bottom up: frame rails, crossmembers, suspension, brake-line runs and wheel wells. It dwells a few minutes and is never left to dry.
  3. Rinse. A thorough rinse with the undercarriage wand, front to back, until the run-off is clear.
  4. Capture. The car sits on a containment mat the whole time. The salty water is collected and disposed of properly, never sent into a storm drain.

The degreaser leaves a thin film of corrosion inhibitors behind, according to its maker, which helps slow rust. The limits are the same honest ones as any wash: it doesn't remove rust that's already there, it isn't an undercoating or a rustproofing treatment, and it cleans what water and a degreaser can reach without opening sealed panels or closed frame sections. If we see something underneath that looks like damage, we'll show you a photo. We don't sell repairs.

Rinsing it yourself? Pick an above-freezing day, aim into the wheel wells and along the frame rails, not just the lower panels, and keep going until the water runs clear. Mind where the water goes. Salt Lake County doesn't allow salt or degreaser run-off into storm drains, which is why every flush runs over a containment mat.

Spring cleanup

When the storms stop, the salt doesn't leave on its own. Magnesium chloride keeps pulling moisture from the air into spring. A thorough end-of-season cleanup covers:

  • A last flush underneath, so the winter's buildup isn't left in seams through the warm months.
  • Wheels inside and out, including the barrels and around the lug seats, where brine dries into white crust.
  • Door jambs, lower door edges and rocker seams, and the drain holes in them, so water can get out.
  • The paint, checked for chips on the hood, bumper and rockers, so exposed metal can be touched up before it spreads under the paint.
  • An inspection underneath, including the brake lines. Scaly or flaking corrosion on a brake line is a job for a mechanic.
  • Protection topped up, if a ceramic sealant or ceramic soap has worn down over the winter.

Questions

Is magnesium chloride worse for my car than regular road salt?

It depends on the conditions, and that's the honest answer. Both are chlorides and both corrode steel and aluminum. In Colorado's tests, magnesium chloride was more corrosive in humid conditions and sodium chloride more corrosive under immersion and in dry conditions. Magnesium chloride's practical problem is that it turns back into a liquid whenever the air is above about 33% humidity, so it stays wet on the metal longer. Either way, washing it off is the fix.

Does an automatic car wash with an underbody spray do enough?

It helps, and it's far better than nothing. Any rinse dilutes salt. What a short spray can't do is cut the oily road film that holds salt in seams, or spend long on the frame rails, brake-line runs and wheel wells. That's why an undercarriage wash uses a degreaser first and a long rinse after.

Will a ceramic coating protect the underside of my car?

No. Paint coatings are made for paint, wheels and glass, and that's where they help winter grime rinse off. The frame, brake lines and suspension need washing. The Winter Salt Flush cleans the underside, but it isn't an undercoating or a rustproofing treatment.

Is it a problem to wash my car below freezing?

Water can freeze in door seals, locks and brake parts, and on the driveway. Wait for an above-freezing day and give the car time to drain. Flushes are done only on above-freezing days; if the forecast turns too cold, we'll text you the evening before to move the appointment.

My car is only a few years old. Does any of this matter yet?

Corrosion builds up over years, so the habits matter most early. In the federal brake-line investigation, failures were rare before 8 years of service and climbed with age in salt states. Factory zinc and coatings get used up by salt exposure, and washing it off slows how fast that happens.
