Lab Notes · Paint depth gauging

Paint depth gauging: why we measure before polishing.

By Johnny Trang, founder · Updated November 2026 · 11 min read

Polishing works by removing clear coat, and a car only has a few dozen microns of it, so we measure how much paint each panel has before any of it comes off. A paint-depth gauge also shows which panels were repainted, filled or polished before, which decides how much correction each panel can safely take.

What is actually on your car

Factory paint is not one coat. It is a stack of thin layers, each with its own job. A carmaker paint researcher's overview lists them from the metal up:1

  • Phosphate, a conversion layer on bare steel or aluminum that helps corrosion protection.
  • Electrocoat (e-coat), a primer deposited electrically onto the whole body, mainly for corrosion protection.
  • Primer (often called primer surfacer), which smooths the e-coat, protects it from light and helps the color coat stick.
  • Basecoat, the color and any metallic or pearl flakes.
  • Clear coat, the top layer, which gives gloss and protects the basecoat from UV light.

Each layer is measured in microns (µm), thousandths of a millimeter. In the U.S. you will also see mils, thousandths of an inch: 1 mil is 25.4 µm. Two independent industry sources give similar numbers:

The carmaker total is our sum of the listed layers. Real cars spread wider than any single table. A gauge maker's automotive guide puts most factory paint at 100–180 µm (4–7 mils) with clear coat at 35–50 µm (1.5–2.0 mils), and notes that some roofs carry only about 75 µm (3 mils) in total.3 A paint maker's refinish guide gives about 90–120 µm for an OEM finish.4 So the useful number is never "the" thickness. It is this car's thickness, panel by panel.

Plastic parts carry a similar top stack. Bumper covers skip the e-coat and get a conductive or adhesion primer instead, then basecoat and clear coat at about the same thicknesses as the steel body.1

Why clear coat is finite

Swirls and light scratches are tiny valleys in the clear coat. Polish does not fill them. The abrasive levels the surface by cutting the surrounding clear coat down toward the bottom of the defect, so light reflects evenly again. That is the whole trade: every bit of gloss you get back from correction is paid for in clear coat. We go through the optics on The science: clear coat.

The clear coat also carries the UV absorbers that protect the color underneath.1 I-CAR, the collision-repair training body, warns that taking off too much clear can lead to hazing, discoloration, peeling and finish failure, and that how much can safely come off depends on how much was put on in the first place.5 One gauge maker reports that most carmakers advise removing no more than about 8 µm (0.3 mil) of clear.3 Against a 35–50 µm layer, that is a small budget.

Clear coat also does not grow back, and you cannot add it with a bottle. Restoring it means refinishing: in a body shop the panel gets primer surfacer, basecoat and clear coat sprayed again. The factory e-coat is skipped in repair because it is so hard to apply outside a plant.2 A ceramic coating is not a replacement either. It is a separate layer that sits on top of the clear coat, which is why the paint is corrected and measured before a coating goes on. More on that in How to choose a ceramic coating.

How much does a polishing step remove?

Honest answer: it depends, and we could not find a published, controlled measurement that puts a single number on it. What we found were forum and blog estimates that disagree with each other, so we left them out. Removal depends on the paint itself, the pad, the compound or polish, machine speed, pressure, and how long the pad stays on one spot.

What is well established is the direction. A cutting compound removes more than a finishing polish. Two steps remove more than one. Repeated passes in one spot add up. And the amount taken by one careful step can be small next to the scatter between two gauge readings, which is one reason a gauge maker's guide says paint should be measured regularly during buffing, since it is "virtually impossible to see how much paint" has been removed.3 The same guide notes that carmakers now specify measuring paint before and after any wet sanding or buffing.3

How a paint-depth gauge works

Most handheld gauges never "see" the paint at all. They measure the distance from the probe tip to the metal underneath, and because the probe rests on the paint, that distance is the paint thickness. ASTM D7091 describes these gauges as measuring the gap between the base metal and the probe.6 Which physics the gauge uses depends on the metal.

On steel: magnetic induction. Steel is ferromagnetic. The probe makes a magnetic field and senses how much flux passes into the steel. The closer the steel, the stronger the flux, so flux density at the probe tip maps directly to the distance from the steel.7 The standards for this case are ASTM D7091 and ISO 2178.6,8

On aluminum and other non-ferrous metals: eddy current. Aluminum is not magnetic, but it conducts electricity. A coil in the probe carries a high-frequency alternating current. Its changing field induces small circulating currents, called eddy currents, in the metal, and those currents make their own opposing field that the probe senses. The closer the metal, the stronger the effect.7 ISO 2360 covers this method for non-conductive coatings, paint included, on non-magnetic conductive metals.8 Many automotive gauges carry both sensors and pick the method for the metal they touch.3

On plastic and composite: ultrasound. Magnetic and eddy-current gauges only work over metal.9 A bumper cover, mirror shell or side cladding is plastic,9 and fiberglass panels are the same problem.3 Over those, an ultrasonic gauge sends a short sound pulse through a drop of couplant (water or a gel) into the paint. Part of the pulse reflects at every boundary where the material changes, and the gauge converts travel time into thickness: round-trip time divided by two, times the speed of sound in the coating.9,10 Some ultrasonic gauges can split the total into up to three layers. Thin adhesion promoter and primer often read as one layer with the basecoat, while the clear coat usually shows as its own layer.9

ISO 2808, the general standard on paint film thickness, describes the methods for measuring wet and dry paint films and gives the field of use and precision for each.11

One point matters for detailing: magnetic and eddy-current gauges read the whole stack. They cannot tell clear coat from basecoat or primer. A reading of 120 µm does not mean 120 µm of clear. It means the metal is 120 µm away, and the clear coat is some fraction of that. That is why we read gauge numbers as comparisons across the car, not as a direct count of clear coat left.6,7

What the readings reveal

A gauge maker's guide puts it simply: readings across one panel should vary only a little, much thicker areas may mean rework, and thinner areas may be a cause for concern.3 In practice, readings tell us five things.

  • Repaint. A panel that reads well above its neighbors has usually had extra paint added on top.3 That is not always a body shop. The carmaker overview notes that 5–10% of vehicles are repainted in the factory itself, basecoat and clear or primer, basecoat and clear, before they ever ship.1 Either way, it is a reason to test before polishing that panel.
  • Body filler. Filler is non-magnetic, so over steel it reads as extra coating. A reading far above the rest of the car, or one past the gauge's range, can mean filler under the paint.3 A paint maker's refinish guide treats 300 µm or more as too thick and says it should be removed or sanded back.4
  • Thin areas and edges. Edges, body lines and tight curves are where gauge readings are least reliable,7 so we treat them with the most caution and the least aggressive work.
  • Previous correction. A panel that reads well below its neighbors may have been polished or wet-sanded before, or may simply have been sprayed thinner at the plant. Roofs and horizontal panels can be thinner from the factory.3 We can't tell those two apart from a number alone, so a thin panel is treated as thin either way.
  • Plastic parts after repair. On cars with radar sensors behind the bumper, paint thickness matters for the sensors too. One carmaker's 2026 position statement limits refinish on ADAS-equipped bumper fascias to 13 mils (about 330 µm) and requires a non-metallic film thickness tool to check it.12,10 A bumper that reads far above the hood is one we look at closely before polishing.

Measurement error, and why one reading isn't enough

No gauge reads perfectly, and the errors are worth knowing because the polishing budget is so small.

  • Instrument accuracy. Electronic automotive gauges are typically specified at about ±1–3% of the reading.3 On a 120 µm reading, ±3% is ±3.6 µm, close to half of the 8 µm removal guideline above. One number alone can't show a few microns of change.
  • The surface and the metal. These gauges are sensitive to surface roughness, curvature, how thick the metal is, the type of metal, and distance from an edge.7 Body lines, tight curves and panel edges are where readings are least trustworthy.
  • The paint itself varies. ASTM D7091 notes that coating thickness can vary widely across a surface, so a single-point reading may not represent the actual coating.6
  • Drift and technique. Lower-cost gauges drift and should be checked before use each day, and the probe should be lifted clear of the surface between readings rather than dragged.3 Gauges are calibrated against thickness standards traceable to a national lab such as NIST.7

The fix for all of these is the same: many readings, in a pattern, compared with each other. Several readings across each panel show its spread. Comparing panels shows the car's own baseline, so a repaint or a thin area stands out against the car rather than against a number from the internet. Re-reading the same spots during correction shows the trend, which is more reliable than any single before-and-after pair.

How the readings set the correction grade

Two things decide the grade: what the paint looks like under inspection lights, and what the gauge says it can spare. Lumen has three grades:

  1. Grade I, polish. One machine-polishing step for gloss and clarity. It removes light haze and the finest swirls. Correction before a coating is optional; this is the lightest grade if you want one.
  2. Grade II, one-step compound and polish. An all-in-one compound and polish on every panel, for light-to-moderate swirls and wash marring.
  3. Grade III, two-step. A cutting compound step for defect removal, then a finishing polish for full clarity and depth. It takes up to 2 days, so it is drop-off only.

Every panel is gauged before we polish and checked again as we go, at every grade. The grade is set for the car, but the work is set panel by panel. A thick, healthy hood can take the full step. A thin edge or a previously corrected door gets a lighter pad and polish, or is left alone. We use the least aggressive pad and polish that does the job and take off only what's needed, measured in microns. You get panel-by-panel paint-depth readings and 50/50 photos, half corrected and half not.

Some scratches are deeper than the clear coat can spare. Polishing can soften how they look, but levelling them fully would take more clear than is safe to remove. The readings are what make that call honestly.

The test spot

Before committing a whole car, we polish a small test spot. It shows two things at once: how the paint responds to a given pad and polish, and what the result looks like on your own paint. Paint responds differently from car to car, and factory paint and refinished panels on the same car can respond differently too, so a combination that works on one panel can be too mild or too aggressive on the next. Starting gentle and stepping up only if needed keeps removal to what the finish actually requires. The test spot is free, and it is the easiest way to decide between grades.

Questions

Does the gauge tell you how much clear coat is left?

Not directly. Magnetic and eddy-current gauges measure the distance to the metal, so they read the whole paint stack: clear, basecoat, primer and e-coat together. Clear coat is usually around 35–50 µm of a 100–180 µm total. That's why readings are compared across panels and re-checked during polishing rather than read as clear coat alone.

Is a thick reading bad news?

Not necessarily. It usually means paint was added, by a body shop or in the factory itself, where a share of new cars are repainted before shipping. Very high readings over steel can mean body filler. It's information, not a verdict: it tells us to test that panel before polishing it.

Can you measure plastic bumpers?

Not with a magnetic or eddy-current gauge. Those only work over metal. Paint on plastic and fiberglass is measured with an ultrasonic gauge, which times a sound pulse through the paint. Bumper covers are also where many edges and tight curves sit, so they get gentle work either way.

Should I buy a gauge before polishing my own car?

If you plan to machine-polish, a gauge tells you what you can't see. Take several readings per panel, lift the probe between readings, and compare panels on your own car rather than chasing a single number. Readings on edges and tight curves are the least reliable.
