Lab Notes · Coating care

Why ceramic coatings stop beading, and how to bring it back.

By Johnny Trang, owner · Updated November 2026 · 12 min read

A coating usually stops beading because something is sitting on top of it, not because it's gone. Hard-water minerals, road film and soap or wax residue cover the low-energy coating with higher-energy patches, so water spreads and sticks instead of beading and rolling. A proper wash, then a decon matched to the coating maker's pH range, then a topper often brings it back; only a coating that is truly worn through needs to be reapplied.

Why water beads in the first place

A drop of water on a surface is a tug-of-war between three interfaces: solid and air, solid and water, and water and air. Each one has an energy per unit area, called surface energy or surface tension. The two names describe the same quantity, since a joule per square meter equals a newton per meter.3 The balance at the edge of the drop is Young's equation:1

cos θ = (γSV − γSL) / γLV

Here θ is the contact angle, measured through the water where the drop meets the surface. γSV is the solid–vapor energy, γSL the solid–liquid energy, and γLV the surface tension of the water itself, about 72.8 mN/m at 20 °C.2 Water's surface tension is high because its molecules hold on to each other so strongly.

Read the equation this way: the lower the surface energy of the solid, the smaller cos θ gets, and the larger the contact angle.3 A low-energy surface gives water little reason to spread, so the water pulls itself into a bead. Chemists call a contact angle under 90° wetting and anything from 90° to 180° non-wetting.1 One coating maker's technical note puts it in the same terms: over 90° is water-repellent, under 30° is hydrophilic.3

Most ceramic coatings cure from silicon chemistry, such as polysilazane, into a thin glass-like silica network on the clear coat.3 Its low surface energy is what you are seeing when water beads. More on the chemistry is on The science: ceramic coatings.

Static angle vs. roll-off angle

A tall, round bead is only half the story. What owners actually notice is whether drops roll off or sit there. That is a different measurement.

If you push a drop along a surface, its front edge (the advancing angle, θA) and back edge (the receding angle, θR) take different angles. The difference is called contact angle hysteresis.1 A drop on a tilted panel starts to slide when gravity beats the grip from that hysteresis. The classic estimate of that grip is the Furmidge equation:6

m g sin α ≈ k w γLV (cos θR − cos θA)

m is the drop's mass, g is gravity, α is the tilt angle, w is the drop's width, and k is a geometric factor that is usually taken as 1. Researchers still debate exactly what the equation describes, the start of sliding or the friction of a moving drop, but the shape of it is the useful part.6 Grip depends on the gap between the advancing and receding angles, not on how tall the bead looks.

An illustration, with numbers I chose for the arithmetic, not measured from any product: a 20 µL drop (about 0.2 mN of weight) about 3 mm wide. With advancing and receding angles of 110° and 100°, the equation predicts it slides at roughly 11° of tilt. Keep the advancing angle near 100° but let contamination drag the receding angle down to 70°, and the same drop needs about 35° before it moves. The bead can still look round while it clings to the panel.

That is why a coated hood can still make beads that never leave. Small defects and contaminant spots pin the edge of the drop and raise hysteresis.5

Hydrophobic vs. superhydrophobic, and the Wenzel and Cassie states

Chemistry alone has a ceiling. A surface packed with fluorinated groups, a very low-energy chemistry, gives water a contact angle of about 120° when it's flat.4 Superhydrophobic usually means a contact angle above 150° with a sliding angle under 10°, and getting there takes low surface energy plus fine texture.4 Texture changes the picture in two ways:

  • Wenzel state. Water fills the grooves of a rough surface.5 Roughness then exaggerates whatever the chemistry already does: cos θW = r cos θ, where r is the true surface area divided by the flat area. A water-repelling surface gets more repelling, a wetting surface gets more wetting, and drops in this state tend to stick.
  • Cassie state. The drop sits on the tips of the texture with air trapped underneath, like a lotus leaf.4 The apparent angle follows cos θCB = fsl cos θsl − fla, where fsl and fla are the fractions of the drop's base touching solid and air.5 Compared with the Wenzel state it gives a higher angle and lower hysteresis, so drops roll.5

The Cassie state is delicate. Pressure, vibration or even the weight of the drop can push water down into the texture and flip it to the sticky Wenzel state.5 A glossy paint coating is built smooth, so the useful way to think about it is as a smooth, low-energy hydrophobic surface. The plain-language version: its water behavior depends on the chemistry of the top few molecules, which is exactly the part that dirt covers up.

How contamination masks a coating

Young's equation assumes one clean material. A used car's paint is a patchwork. A useful rule for mixed surfaces has the same form as the Cassie equation: the apparent cos θ is roughly the area-weighted average of each patch's cos θ.5

Another illustration, with assumed numbers: say the clean coating gives 100°, and mineral spots give 40°. Cover 30% of the panel with spots and the average works out to cos θ ≈ 0.7 × cos 100° + 0.3 × cos 40° ≈ 0.11, or about 84°. The panel has crossed from non-wetting to wetting, and the coating underneath hasn't changed at all. The spots also pin drop edges, so roll-off suffers even sooner than the static angle does.

What does the covering in real life:

  • Hard-water minerals. Hardness is dissolved calcium and magnesium. The USGS calls 121 to 180 mg/L "hard" and anything above 180 mg/L "very hard".8 Salt Lake City's 2025 water report lists total hardness from 121 mg/L at one treatment plant to as high as 487 mg/L in some city wells.9 When a drop dries, the water leaves and the minerals stay. Calcium carbonate is the scale hard water leaves behind,8 and calcite, its common mineral form, is strongly water-wetting: one molecular study found contact angles from too low to measure up to about 46°, and lab measurements in the literature run as high as 90°.10 Either way, it is a more wettable patch sitting on a less wettable coating.
  • Beads that dry in place. The same coating maker's note points out the catch with strong beading: drops that sit and dry can carry airborne pollutants and leave more acidic residue behind, which is why that maker designed one coating to repel water less, at 75 to 80°.3
  • Soap residue. Surfactants work by lowering the surface tension of water.7 In Young's equation, a smaller γLV means a smaller contact angle, so soapy water spreads on a coating that clean water would bead on. In hard water, calcium and magnesium also clump soap into a scum that settles on surfaces.7 Poor rinsing can make a healthy coating look tired for a day.
  • Wax and gloss layers. Wax or wash-and-wax over a coating hides its beading.11 Whatever you see is the wax's water behavior, not the coating's.
  • Road film and iron. Oily road film and fine grime bond to the top layer. Brake dust leaves iron particles that embed and rust in place, which is why paint can feel rough after a wash.13

Wear vs. masking: how to tell

Masking is a layer on top. Wear is the coating getting thinner or losing its water-repelling surface chemistry. They look the same from the driveway, and the difference matters because masking comes off with a decon and wear doesn't.

The cleanest test is a controlled one, the same idea as a 50/50 polishing photo. Wash and dry the car, then deep-clean half of one panel (decon below) and leave the other half alone. Mist both halves with clean water. If beading and roll-off come back on the cleaned half, you were looking at masking. If the cleaned half still sheets flat and feels grabby, the coating on that panel is likely worn, or was never there to begin with.

Two other clues. Ask what the coating was designed to do: some coatings are built for lower water repellency on purpose, so even sheeting from day one isn't a failure.3 And remember the rating. Lumen's paint coatings are maker-rated up to 24 months (Essential), up to 36 months (Signature), and up to 50 months to five years (Reserve), properly applied and maintained. A coating well past its maker's rating is more likely worn than masked.

Three tests you can do at home

  1. Sheeting. On a washed, cool panel, run a gentle stream from an open hose, no nozzle. A healthy hydrophobic coating pulls the water off in a sheet that breaks away cleanly and leaves few drops behind. Water that hangs in a film, or leaves a lot of drops stuck in place, points to contamination or wear.
  2. Beading and roll-off. Mist a panel with clean water. Look at the drops from the side: round and tall, or flat like lenses? Then look at a vertical panel, like a door. If drops slide down on their own, hysteresis is low. If round beads stay put, something is pinning them.
  3. Slickness. On a clean, dry panel, glide the back of your fingers or a clean microfiber across it. A clean coating feels slick and even. Grabby or gritty spots usually mean bonded contamination on top.

Test with clean water, and rinse well before you judge. Soap left on the panel lowers water's surface tension and will make any coating look worse than it is.7 When I rinse a panel at the walkaround, this is what I'm looking at.

The restoration ladder

Start at the bottom and stop as soon as the beading comes back. Each step up removes more, so there's no reason to skip ahead.

  1. A proper wash. pH-neutral shampoo, a two-bucket hand wash, and a thorough rinse, out of direct sun. A neutral shampoo cleans with lubrication and surfactants rather than chemical force, so it doesn't strip the coating. Skip automatic brush washes and wash-and-wax products on a coated car.
  2. Decontamination, within the maker's pH range. Mineral scale is mostly carbonate, and acid dissolves carbonate: acid plus carbonate gives a salt, carbon dioxide and water, so the mineral goes back into solution and rinses away.11 Iron removers built on thioglycolate reduce iron and carry it off as a purple, soluble complex.13 Check your coating maker's chemical tolerance first. One maker lists its paint coating's pH tolerance as 2 to 11,12 which is why a mild acidic remover is safe on that coating. Follow the product's dwell time, work a panel at a time on a cool surface, never let it dry, and rinse thoroughly.
  3. A topper. A ceramic topper, or the coating maker's own booster, refreshes slickness and beading and adds a thin sacrificial layer that takes the wear instead of the coating. This step only helps once the surface underneath is clean; a topper over mineral film just traps it.
  4. Professional decon. Lumen's Decon & Reseal, done every 6 to 12 months, is a full chemical decon that removes iron fallout and mineral spots, brings back the just-coated beading and self-cleaning, and finishes with a fresh ceramic sealant topper over the coating, film or matte. On coatings I install, the yearly version is the Annual Coating Service: inspection and photos, a decontamination wash with a mineral (acidic) remover to clear clogged, dull spots and bring the beading back, iron removal, a top-up with the coating maker's booster, and an updated warranty record. It is the same service as Decon & Reseal, and it's part of how the warranty on those coatings works.
  5. Reapplication, when it's truly worn. If the half-panel test shows nothing left to uncover, or etched spots have to be polished out, the answer is a new coating. A coating locks in whatever is under it, so the paint is polished first. On a sedan, coatings start at $500 for the coating, plus Grade I correction from $399. How to choose a ceramic coating covers which one fits how you drive.

What to expect in Utah

Three local conditions stack up against beading here.

  • Hard water. Salt Lake City's own tap water runs from hard to very hard depending on the source,8,9 and sprinklers, especially on untreated secondary irrigation water, are a common source of spots on cars. Keeping sprinklers pointed away from the car does more for beading than any product. For spot-free final rinses I use deionized water, which leaves almost nothing behind when it dries.
  • Altitude and sun. The NOAA solar monitoring station near the Salt Lake City airport sits at 1,288 m, about 4,226 feet,15 and UV intensity increases with altitude because there is less atmosphere to absorb it.14 Strong sun also dries drops fast, and mineral spots that sit and bake and re-wet again and again concentrate and can etch.
  • Winter roads. Salt and magnesium chloride brine leave a film that holds grime. It is a neutral salt, so it rinses away with water rather than being neutralized.

To be straight about the limits: I didn't find published data that puts a number on how much altitude or UV shortens a given car coating's life, so I won't guess one. The ratings above are the makers' own, and real life depends on how the car is driven, washed and stored. What I can say is that minerals land on a coating all season here, so it's worth ruling out masking with the half-panel test before deciding a coating is worn.

Questions

My coating stopped beading. Is it gone?

Usually not. Most often it is masked by hard-water minerals, road film or soap residue sitting on top. Wash it, then deep-clean half of one panel with a mineral remover that fits your coating maker's pH range and compare the two halves with a mist of clean water. If the cleaned half beads again, the coating is still there.

Is sheeting instead of beading a bad sign?

Not by itself. Water that pulls off a panel in a clean sheet and leaves few drops behind is a good sign. Some coatings are even designed to repel water less, so fewer drops sit and dry into spots. The warning sign is water that hangs in a film or leaves drops stuck in place.

Will an acidic cleaner hurt my ceramic coating?

Check your coating maker's chemical tolerance first. One maker lists its paint coating at pH 2 to 11, so a mild acidic mineral remover is within range on that coating. Use one made for coated cars, follow the label's dwell time, work on a cool panel, never let it dry, and rinse thoroughly.

Can a topper fix a coating that stopped beading?

Only after the surface is clean. A topper refreshes slickness and adds a thin sacrificial layer, but put over mineral film it covers the problem instead of removing it. Wash first, decon if the half-panel test says you need it, then top it.

Someone else coated my car. Can you bring the beading back?

Yes. Refresh Washes and Decon & Reseal are built for coated cars. Tell me who installed it, because some coating warranties require an annual inspection by the original installer.
