Lab Notes · Coating care

How to wash a ceramic-coated car without wearing it down.

By Johnny Trang, owner · Updated November 2026 · 12 min read

Wait out the cure time your coating's maker gives, then hand wash with a pH-neutral shampoo, a clean mitt and plenty of lubrication, and dry with a blower or a plush towel. Skip automatic brush washes and strong touchless pre-soaks, keep wax off the coating, and plan on a mineral and iron decon about once a year to bring the beading back.

What you're actually washing

A ceramic coating is a thin, glass-like layer, mostly silica, that cures on top of the clear coat (see The science: ceramic coatings). Its surface has low surface energy, so water can't spread out across it. Chemists measure that with the contact angle, the angle a droplet makes where it meets the surface. Above 90°, a liquid is non-wetting: it sits up in beads instead of flattening into a sheet.14 That beading, and the slickness that comes with it, is what makes a coated car easier to clean.

Two things follow from that. First, a coating is tough, but it is not a force field. It sits on the same clear coat that swirls when you rub grit across it, and the coating itself can be marred the same way. Second, most of what people call a "failed" coating is a coating that is still there but buried under road film and hard-water minerals. Washing well keeps it working. Washing badly hides it, then wears it.

Makers rate coatings for years, not months, but only with proper care. One professional paint coating is maker-rated up to 5 years "when properly maintained," and its maker says longevity depends on driving, environment and washing habits.2 Another is maker-rated up to 5 years, with a stated range of 2 to 5 years.3 The coatings I install are maker-rated up to 24 months (Essential), up to 36 months (Signature) and up to 50 months to five years (Reserve). How you wash is a big part of whether a car gets there.

Before the first wash: the cure window

A coating goes on as a liquid and hardens as it reacts with moisture in the air. Until it has cured, water, soap and other chemicals can mark it or slow the bond. How long that takes depends on the product, so the only reliable number is the one its maker prints. Four makers' published instructions show how much they differ:

  • One says to keep the surface out of direct sun and away from water for the first 12 hours.1
  • Another says the car should stay indoors for 12 hours after application.2
  • A third says keep it dry for at least 1 hour, 24 hours for the most reliable result, and avoid detergents and chemicals for 5 days.3
  • A fourth says keep it dry for 24 hours, no washing with chemicals for at least 7 days, and that some coatings need 2 to 4 days to fully cure.4

So "wait a week" is a reasonable rule of thumb, but it is not a fact about every coating. If rain or a sprinkler hits the car during the early window, don't wipe it hard. One maker says to gently wipe water off during the first 24 hours.3 I tell you the cure times for the coating I used before I hand back the keys, and I check in about 30 days later to look for high spots and confirm the cure.

pH: what the soap is doing

pH runs from 0 to 14, and each whole step is a tenfold change in acidity. Acids and alkalis dissolve different kinds of dirt, which is why detailers use more than one cleaner. Here is how each one fits a coated car.

  • pH-neutral shampoo, for every routine wash. It cleans with surfactants and lubrication rather than chemical force. Surfactant molecules surround oily soil and hold it in the water so it rinses away. The coating makers I cite say the same thing for their own products: wash with a pH-neutral shampoo and avoid harsh chemicals.1,4
  • Mild acid, for minerals, now and then. Hard-water spots are mostly calcium and magnesium carbonate. Acid dissolves carbonate and lets it rinse away. That is the job of a mineral (acidic) decon on a coating whose beading has gone flat. One maker rates its coating to tolerate pH 2 to 11, which is why a mild acid at the label's dwell time is safe on it.1
  • Alkaline cleaners, for heavy grease, where it's needed. High pH breaks down oils and road film. On a coated car, keep strong alkaline cleaners on wheels, tires and wheel wells, not on the paint every week. One coating maker's care guide says to avoid heavy degreasers on coatings.4

What strong cleaners wear down first is the thin topper on top. Over many washes, they wear the coating too. The pH toolbox, with sources, is on The science: pH.

Why automatic brush washes cause marring

Paint scientists separate two kinds of damage. A scratch fractures the clear coat. A mar is a shallow groove that deforms the clear coat without fracturing it. Car-wash scratches are mars. A review from automotive coating researchers describes them as typically only a few microns wide and deep, caused by automatic car wash brushes and by home car washing.5 A single mar is hard to see. In large numbers they lower the gloss of the clear coat, and they show up as a web of fine lines on a bright day when you look at the paint at a shallow angle.5 Researchers at the National Institute of Standards and Technology say these shallow deformations are most often the result of car washing.6

The industry takes this seriously enough to have a standard test for it. ISO 20566 simulates machine washing in the lab with a rotating brush and synthetic dirt, under conditions designed to match a real car wash, to measure a paint's car-wash resistance.7 The review notes that where automatic washes often use stiff plastic bristles, owners are more concerned with car-wash scratches and the loss of gloss that comes with them.5

The physics is basic tribology, the study of surfaces sliding against each other. In Archard's model of wear, the volume worn away is proportional to the load pressing two surfaces together and the distance they slide, and inversely proportional to the hardness of the surface being worn.8 A brush wash maximizes the first two. Its brushes spin across every panel, wash after wash, and they carry grit from the cars before yours. The same review notes that wiping damage happens at a much higher rate when the wiping material is embedded with a contaminant such as road grit.5 A coating's slick surface lowers friction, and one maker says that reduces the likelihood of wash swirls.2 It doesn't change the fact that sand dragged across paint cuts grooves in whatever it touches.

That is why the care card for every coating I install says no automatic brush washes, and why the warranty excludes damage from improper washing.

Touchless washes and strong pre-soaks

A touchless wash avoids the brushes, so it can't drag grit across the paint the way a brush can. The trade is chemistry. With nothing touching the car, the wash has to do the work chemically. A carwash-industry article on touch-free chemistry explains that these systems need stronger surfactants, plus stronger acids and alkalis, to make up for the lack of friction. It also describes a common two-step process: an acid-based pre-soak first, then an alkaline one.9

The bay doesn't post the pH of what it sprays, and you can't control the dwell time or whether it dries on a hot panel. Strong cleaners wear down the topper that keeps a coating slick, and then the coating itself takes more of the wear. A touchless wash now and then, in winter, to get salt off is a reasonable compromise when a hand wash isn't possible. As a weekly habit, a pH-neutral hand wash is gentler.

The hand wash, step by step

This is the order I use on coated paint. The idea behind every step is the same: get the grit off before anything touches the paint, keep lubrication high, and never drag dirt across the surface.

  1. Work in the shade, on cool paint. Water and soap that dry on a hot panel leave spots. Early morning or evening is easier in a Utah summer.
  2. Wheels and tires first, with their own tools. Wheels carry sand and brake dust. Use a separate mitt or brush for them so that grit never reaches the paint.10
  3. Pre-rinse, top down. Rinse the loose dirt off before you touch the car. With a pressure washer, I keep the tip at least 12 inches back with a wide 40° fan, and never aim it at an edge, seam or badge.
  4. Foam with a pH-neutral shampoo. A pre-wash foam softens and lifts dirt so more of it rinses off before contact. Let it dwell, don't let it dry, and rinse again.
  5. Two buckets. One bucket holds soapy wash water. The other holds plain rinse water. A grit screen in the bottom of each helps keep settled dirt away from the mitt.
  6. Wash one panel at a time, top down. Use a clean, soft mitt loaded with suds and light pressure, in straight lines. Rinse the mitt in the rinse bucket after each panel, then reload it from the soap bucket. Do the lower panels last; they carry the most grit.
  7. Final rinse. Rinse thoroughly from the top down. For my spot-free final rinses, I use deionized water, which leaves almost nothing behind when it dries. See The science: hard water.
  8. Dry without rubbing. Use a blower to push water out of edges, mirrors and seams, then a plush towel laid on and blotted or pulled gently for whatever is left.
  9. Topper, if it's due. A spray topper made for coatings, applied to clean, dry paint, refreshes slickness and beading. More below.

Drying, and why it matters in the Salt Lake Valley

Letting a car air dry, or driving it around the block, leaves water marks from the minerals in hard water.10 The U.S. Geological Survey classifies 121 to 180 mg/L of hardness (as calcium carbonate) as hard and anything above 180 mg/L as very hard.11 Valley tap water and especially sprinkler water can leave spots fast. When a drop dries, the water leaves and the calcium and magnesium stay. On a coated car those minerals sit on top of the coating and dull its beading. Left to bake and re-wet again and again, they can etch.

A filtered blower is the gentlest way to dry because nothing touches the paint. A plush microfiber drying towel is the next step down. Lay it on and blot, or draw it slowly across the panel with no pressure, rather than scrubbing.10

Toppers and boosters

A topper is a thin, easy-to-apply ceramic layer that goes on over the coating. It refreshes slickness and water beading, and it adds a thin sacrificial layer that takes the wear instead of the coating. A booster is the coating maker's own top-up product, matched to that coating. Makers set their own intervals: one says to use its topper every 3 months or any time you like,3 and another suggests topping up with its own products to boost water repellency.1

Two cautions. Use a product sold for coated paint, not a wax or a wash-and-wax. And apply it to clean, dry paint in the shade, following its label. A topper over dirty paint seals the dirt in.

How often to wash in Utah

One coating maker's care guide suggests a light wash or a waterless wash every 2 weeks, or whenever the car is dirty.4 That is a fair baseline. Utah adds two seasons that change it.

Dust season. The Utah Geological Survey notes that the state historically sees frequent dust storms from March to May, with a second peak in the fall. Much of the dust reaching the Wasatch Front comes from dry lakebeds in western and southwestern Utah, and it is clay and silt-sized sediment, 2 to 50 micrometers across, which can carry minerals and metals.12 Those are the particles that cause marring when they get dragged across paint. After a dust storm, rinse before you touch the car, and wash sooner rather than wiping it.

Salt season. UDOT has reported using roughly 236,000 tons of road salt and about 1.8 million gallons of brine in a typical year.13 Chloride ions are the main cause of steel degradation near roads.15 A coating protects the paint it covers. It does nothing for the frame, brake lines and suspension underneath. In winter, rinse the salt film off the paint every week or two when temperatures allow, and flush the underside after storms and at the end of the season. How that works is on the Winter Salt Flush page.

Between those seasons, wash when it's dirty. Bird droppings and bugs are the exception: get them off the same day. Soak them with your wash and lift them off rather than scrubbing.

When the beading fades: decon and maintenance

Months of driving leave two kinds of buildup that a normal wash doesn't remove. Hard-water minerals settle on the coating and flatten the beading. Iron fallout from brakes lands on the paint and embeds. A coating that sheets water slowly is usually clogged, not gone.

The fix is chemical, not abrasive. A mineral (acidic) remover dissolves the carbonate layer, an iron remover dissolves embedded iron, and a booster or topper goes back on. One maker suggests decontaminating with its iron remover every 3 to 6 months.3 Polishing is a last resort, because it takes off coating and clear coat with it. A coating also isn't clayed or polished as routine care.4

Here is how this works on the coatings I install, as listed on the paint and coatings page:

  • Care card, with every coating. pH-neutral soap only, no automatic brush washes, dry with a blower or plush towel, bird droppings off the same day, and sprinklers pointed away from the car. Your first maintenance visit is booked before I leave.
  • Annual Coating Service, once a year. Inspection and photos, a decontamination wash with a mineral (acidic) remover to clear clogged, dull spots and bring the beading back, iron removal, a top-up with the coating maker's booster, and your warranty record updated. The warranty depends on it being done within 30 days of each anniversary, with me or as documented equivalent maintenance.
  • Refresh Wash, between visits. A finish-safe hand wash with careful, edge-safe drying, iron fallout gently dissolved where needed, and, on coated cars, a ceramic topper included. Beading and sheeting are checked each time.
  • Decon & Reseal, every 6 to 12 months. The same service as the Annual Coating Service: a full chemical decon for iron fallout and mineral spots, then a fresh ceramic sealant topper. A ceramic sealant on its own is maker-rated up to 6 months or 50 washes.

Coated by someone else? Those services work on any coating. Tell me who installed it, because some coating warranties need the original installer's annual inspection. From about November to March, mobile visits cover rinseless washes and undercarriage flushes rather than full exterior washes.

If your car is wrapped, matte or has paint protection film, the rules change: no gloss products on matte, and no pressure at film edges. That is covered on Wrap, matte & PPF care. Still choosing a coating? Start with how to choose a ceramic coating for how you drive.

Questions

How long after a ceramic coating can I wash my car?

It depends on the coating, so follow its maker's instructions. Published instructions range from 12 hours away from water to no washing with chemicals for at least 7 days. A gentle rinse is usually fine sooner than soap. I tell you the cure times for your coating before I hand back the keys.

Is a touchless car wash safe for a ceramic coating?

Safer than a brush wash, because nothing drags grit across the paint. But touchless washes make up for the lack of contact with stronger acids, alkalis and surfactants, which wear down toppers and, over time, the coating. Use one now and then, for example to get winter salt off, and hand wash with a pH-neutral shampoo the rest of the time.

Can I use dish soap on a coated car?

No. Dish soap is made to cut grease off plates, not to clean paint, and household cleaners can strip the protection on top of a coating. Use a pH-neutral car shampoo or your coating maker's own wash.

My coating stopped beading. Is it gone?

Usually not. Most of the time hard-water minerals and road film are sitting on top of it. A mineral (acidic) decon, iron removal and a booster or topper usually bring the beading back. That is what the Annual Coating Service and Decon & Reseal do.

Should I wax over my ceramic coating?

No. Wax sits on top and hides the coating's own beading and slickness. If you want to refresh the surface, use a topper made for coated paint or the coating maker's booster.
