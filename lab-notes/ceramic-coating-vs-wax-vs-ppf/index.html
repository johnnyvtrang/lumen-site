Lab Notes · Paint protection compared

Ceramic coating, ceramic sealant, wax or PPF: what each one actually does.

By Johnny Trang, owner · Updated November 2026 · 12 min read

Wax, ceramic sealant and ceramic coating are chemical layers that make paint glossier, slicker and easier to wash, and they differ mainly in how hard they cure and how long their makers rate them: weeks for wax, months for a ceramic sealant, years for a coating. Paint protection film (PPF) is different in kind: a thick, clear urethane film that physically absorbs rock chips, which none of the chemical layers can do. Many cars end up with two of them, film on the parts that take impacts and a coating over everything.

Start with scale: how thick each layer is

Most of the confusion about these products goes away once you picture their thickness next to your paint. A factory paint system is usually 4–7 mils thick, or about 100–180 microns (µm), and the clear coat on top of it is usually 35–50 µm.7 A micron is a thousandth of a millimeter.

  • Wax and ceramic sealant are wiped on and mostly buffed off. What stays is a thin film, and makers rarely publish its thickness.
  • Ceramic coating cures into a hard layer that one maker specifies at up to 2 µm per layer,5 a small fraction of the clear coat under it.
  • Paint protection film is a different order of magnitude. One maker's film is 193 µm without its liner (±10%): a 12 µm top coat, 152 µm of polyurethane and 28 µm of adhesive.8 That is about four to five times the thickness of the clear coat, and as thick as some entire factory paint systems.

Thickness isn't everything, but it sets the ceiling on what a layer can do. A film thinner than a scratch can't stop that scratch. A layer thicker than the whole paint system can take a stone strike that would have reached primer.

Wax: a soft film that sits on top

Car wax was traditionally sold as wax mixed with petroleum solvents, to add gloss.1 The classic ingredient, carnauba, comes from the leaves and buds of a Brazilian palm. Its main grades are made up mostly of long, saturated fatty acids and alcohols with 24 to 32 carbon atoms; it is hard, brittle and insoluble in water.3

That chemistry explains how wax behaves. It doesn't react with the paint. It sits on the clear coat as a film held by weak physical attraction, and its long, oily carbon chains repel water, so the paint beads and looks deeper and wetter. Because nothing cures or crosslinks, wax is soft. Detergent, heat and friction wear it away, and it can't resist a scratch.

Durability is short. One carnauba paste wax is maker-rated for 8–12 weeks, washed and maintained regularly.4 At Lumen, the Refresh level washes with a wax soap, which leaves a little gloss and water beading between washes and nothing more.

Wax is good at: gloss, a warm look on dark paint, low cost, easy reapplication. Wax is not: long-lasting, chemically tough, or protection against marring or chips.

Ceramic sealant: a synthetic layer measured in months

"Ceramic sealant" is a product category, not a single chemistry. These are hand-applied or spray-applied synthetic polymer layers that makers describe as using ceramic technology, meaning silicon-based chemistry related to coatings. Most makers don't publish their formulas, so I won't pretend to know what's in each bottle. What they share is that they bond more durably than wax and are rated in months or washes, not years.

For example, one maker rates its ceramic sealant up to 6 months, and another rates its ceramic sealant for over 50 washes.4 Lumen's Radiance level applies a ceramic sealant by hand, panel by panel, after a full decontamination; it is maker-rated up to 6 months or 50 washes. The step below it, Renew, uses a ceramic soap its maker rates up to 3 months, and the Halo upgrade is a spray coating maker-rated up to 12–18 months.

A ceramic sealant is a good fit when you want real water beading and easier washing for a season, the paint doesn't need correction, or you aren't keeping the car long. It's also how a coating gets topped up: a thin ceramic sealant over a coating takes the wear and refreshes the beading.

Ceramic coating: a thin layer of glass

A true ceramic coating is built on silicon chemistry, often polysilazane or siloxane resins. Polysilazane is a polymer chain of alternating silicon and nitrogen atoms. As it cures, it reacts with the air and converts to silica (SiO2), the same compound as glass. Infrared spectra taken during the cure show the silicon-nitrogen signal falling while the silicon-oxygen signal rises, which is the chemistry of a liquid turning into a glass-like film.1

The cured surface has low surface energy, so water can't spread out and pulls into beads. Chemists measure this as the contact angle, the angle a droplet makes with the surface: above 90° the surface is non-wetting.2 One coating maker lists a water contact angle of 110° for its product.5

Two things follow from this. First, a coating is hard and chemically tougher than wax, so it lasts years instead of weeks: one professional paint coating is maker-rated up to 5 years, properly applied and maintained.4 Second, it is thin and transparent, so it locks in whatever is under it. Swirls under a coating stay there for the life of the coating. That's why the paint is always polished before I coat it, and why paint depth is measured on every panel first, so only what's needed comes off.

What "9H" means, and what it doesn't

Coating labels often say "9H." That refers to the pencil hardness test, a lab method in which graphite pencils from 9B (softest) to 9H (hardest) are drawn across a coating in a defined way. The rating is the hardest pencil that doesn't damage the surface.6 It's a real lab test, but a pencil lead pushed across the surface isn't a rock hitting the car at highway speed, hardness on its own isn't the same thing as scratch resistance, and the number doesn't tell you how many years the coating will last. What the test can and can't show on a coating a couple of microns thick is in How to choose a ceramic coating.

Paint protection film: a thick, elastic shield

PPF is a clear, self-adhesive polyurethane film. One maker's data sheet describes three working layers: an acrylic adhesive that holds the film to the paint, a thick polyurethane body, and an elastomeric clear top coat that, in the maker's words, lets light scratches in the surface "heal" over time.8

The polyurethane body is what matters for chips. It is tough and stretchy: that maker reports a tensile elongation over 300% and tear strength over 60 kN/m.8 When a stone hits, the film deforms and spreads the energy instead of letting it crack the paint underneath.

Chip resistance has a standard test. In the gravelometer test (SAE J400 / ASTM D3170), a coated panel is mounted in a chamber and air pressure throws a measured charge of gravel at it, about a pint, roughly 300 stones. Loose fragments are pulled off with tape and the chips are counted and graded by size.9 The film data sheet above reports "no detrimental effect" after 4.73 L of gravel at 70 psi and −29 °C, and no visible effect after 30-minute soaks in gasoline, diesel, motor oil, acetone and isopropyl alcohol.8

Film has its own limits. It can be cut by a sharp object, its edges need careful washing, strong solvents can attack it or its adhesive, and it costs more because it is a material plus skilled installation labor. Lumen offers PPF through a local install partner; I handle the prep and the aftercare.

What each one protects against

"Protection" means different things for different threats. Here is how the four compare on the ones that matter in Utah.

Sun and UV

Modern clear coats weather mainly by photooxidation: UV light starts free-radical reactions that break the polymer network. Clear coats are formulated with UV absorbers, which soak up UV, and hindered-amine light stabilizers, which mop up the free radicals. Researchers at an automaker's lab showed those additives can slow the breakdown dramatically, and that a base coat/clear coat finish can look fine until it fails suddenly.11 UV intensity also rises with altitude because there is less air above you to absorb it, and snow reflects much of the UV that reaches it.12 The Salt Lake Valley sits above 4,000 feet and has snow on the ground for part of the year.

A protective layer on top adds a sacrificial surface. In one coating maker's accelerated weathering test, coated panels lost gloss nearly 25% more slowly than uncoated ones.1 Wax helps briefly; a ceramic sealant for months; a coating for years. Film is itself rated for weathering: one maker reports its film kept 90% of its gloss after outdoor accelerated exposure equivalent to 3.5 years.8 None of these replaces parking in shade or a garage.

Bird droppings, bugs and road chemicals

Bird droppings etch clear coat mainly through enzymes, not acid. In lab work published by coating chemists, droppings and a digestive-enzyme stand-in were close to neutral pH, yet they broke the ester and ether links in an acrylic-melamine clear coat, leaving etched, dull spots. Damage was greatest at 60 °C of the temperatures tested.13 A layer on top means the dropping sits on that layer first, which buys time. None of these makes it safe to leave droppings in the sun; wet them and lift them off soon.

Utah winter roads add salt and, in places, magnesium chloride brine. Magnesium chloride is hygroscopic: it pulls moisture out of the air even in low humidity, and its fine spray reaches tight seams, so it keeps a damp, corrosive film on a vehicle long after the storm.15 A slick coating or ceramic sealant makes that grime release more easily in the wash. It doesn't neutralize salt, and it doesn't protect the underbody; only washing it off does.

Hard water and sprinklers

Hard water is water with dissolved calcium and magnesium. The USGS classes 121–180 mg/L (as calcium carbonate) as hard and anything above 180 as very hard.14 Salt Lake City's 2025 water report lists total hardness of 121–198 mg/L at its treatment plants, 157–487 mg/L across its wells, and 189.4 mg/L for water from the Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District.14 When that water dries on a car, the minerals stay behind as spots.

Here is the counterintuitive part: high water repellency doesn't prevent spots. Tall beads that sit on a surface and dry leave their minerals, and anything else they picked up, in neat circles. One coating chemist's technical note argues exactly this, and describes a coating deliberately tuned to a lower contact angle (75–80°) so less water stays behind.1 What a coating does do is keep minerals on its own surface, where an acidic decon can dissolve them, instead of on the clear coat. That's why the same advice holds for every layer: park away from sprinklers, and dry the car after washing.

Wash marring and swirls

Most swirls come from washing and drying. The industry even has a standard lab car wash for it: ISO 20566 rates scratch resistance with a rotating brush and synthetic dirt, and the method can be adapted to test protective films.10 Wax and ceramic sealant add slickness, so a wash mitt glides with less drag, but they are too soft or thin to resist marring for long. A coating may make light wash marring a little less likely, but I couldn't find an independent study that measures how much, and at a couple of microns it can't absorb a scratch deeper than itself. Film's elastomeric top coat is the only layer here whose maker claims light scratches recover.8

Rock chips

Only film. A chemical layer measured in microns can't absorb the energy of a stone, so chip protection comes from the thick, elastic polyurethane of PPF.8,9 On Utah freeways and canyon roads, the front of the car (bumper, hood, mirrors, headlights) faces the stones kicked up by traffic ahead.

How they combine

These aren't competitors so much as layers in a stack, and the order matters: correct, protect, maintain.

  • Film, then coating over the top. PPF on the impact zones, a coating on the rest of the paint, and a coating made for film over the film itself. Film-specific coatings exist because film is softer and more flexible than paint; one is maker-rated up to 24 months on gloss, satin and matte film.4 Lumen's film & matte coating is maker-rated up to 24 months, and film is never polished first.
  • Coating, then a ceramic sealant as a topper. A thin ceramic sealant over a coating refreshes slickness and beading and takes the wear instead of the coating.
  • Coating, then wax: usually not. Wax sits on top and hides the coating's beading and slickness. You'd be trading the coating's surface for wax's.
  • Correction first, always, for a coating on gloss paint. A coating or film over swirls preserves the swirls.

What each one asks of you

  • Wax: reapply every few weeks to months; any decent shampoo is fine, though strong cleaners strip it faster.
  • Ceramic sealant: reapply on its rated interval; wash with a pH-neutral shampoo.
  • Ceramic coating: pH-neutral washes, a topper now and then, and an acidic decon when minerals mute the beading. One coating maker rates its coatings to tolerate pH 2–11, which is why that decon is safe.4 No wax, no harsh degreasers.
  • PPF: pH-neutral, film-safe products; keep pressure-washer spray back from the edges; no strong solvents.

More on why coatings "clog" and how a decon brings them back is on The science page.

How the cost works

The price of each layer is mostly labor, and the labor follows the chemistry. Wax and ceramic sealant go on quickly over a clean car. A coating is unforgiving: because it locks in what's underneath and bonds to what it touches, the paint has to be decontaminated, polished and wiped down to bare clear coat first, and that correction is usually the bigger part of the job. Film is a material cost plus skilled installation labor.

So the honest comparison isn't price per bottle, it's cost per year of the protection you actually want. If you're keeping a car a year, a ceramic sealant or spray coating with a detail may be all it needs. If you're keeping it a decade, a coating makes sense, and film on the front makes sense if chips bother you. At Lumen, a coating on gloss paint is the coating plus the correction grade it goes over. On a sedan, coatings start at $500 for the coating, plus Grade I correction from $399. Lumen's paint coatings are maker-rated: Essential up to 24 months, Signature up to 36 months, Reserve up to 50 months to five years. I confirm the choice and the price with you at a free inspection, under lights, and nothing is added without your OK.

Coatings go on in your garage when you have one, so they cure out of the sun, wind and dust. No garage? I use coatings made for outdoor application and plan the day around the weather. If you're still deciding which coating fits, How to choose a ceramic coating walks through it by vehicle.

Questions

Is a ceramic sealant just a weaker ceramic coating?

They're related, but they're built for different jobs. A ceramic sealant goes on quickly over a clean car and is maker-rated in months or washes. A coating cures into a hard silica layer rated in years, and because it locks in what's underneath, the paint is polished first. A ceramic sealant is also how a coating gets topped up between visits.

Do I need a coating if I have PPF?

Not to protect the paint under the film; the film does that. A coating made for film keeps the film itself slick and easier to wash, and many owners put film on the front and a coating on the rest. Film is never polished before it's coated.

Can I wax over a ceramic coating?

You can, but it usually works against you. The wax sits on top, hides the coating's beading and slickness, and wears off in weeks. A ceramic sealant or ceramic topper over the coating keeps its character instead.

Does a 9H coating stop rock chips?

No. "9H" is a pencil hardness rating, the hardest pencil lead that doesn't mark the surface in a lab test. A stone at highway speed is a very different load, and a layer a couple of microns thick can't absorb it. Chips need paint protection film.

Will a coating stop water spots from my sprinklers?

Not on its own. Local water is hard to very hard, and beads that dry in place still leave their minerals. A coating keeps those minerals on its surface, where a decon can dissolve them, instead of on the clear coat. Moving the car or the sprinkler, and drying after washes, matters more than any product.
