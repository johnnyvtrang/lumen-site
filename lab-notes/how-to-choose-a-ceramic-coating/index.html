Lab Notes · Choosing a coating

How to choose a ceramic coating for how you drive.

By Johnny Trang, owner · Updated November 2026 · 13 min read

Pick the coating for the job the vehicle does, not for the brand on the bottle. A trail or hunting rig needs chemical resistance and a surface mud lets go of; a family car needs slick, easy washing; a show car needs gloss, which mostly comes from correcting the paint before the coating goes on.

What actually differs between coatings

Most ceramic coatings cure into a thin, glass-like silica layer on top of the clear coat. Water beads on it, and dirt has less to grip.1 Past that shared idea, coatings differ in a few ways that matter to you:

  • Chemistry. How the glass-like network forms, and what sits on its surface to make water bead.
  • Layers. Some are one layer. Others are a base layer for strength and chemical resistance with a slick top layer for beading and dirt release.
  • What they're tuned for. Some lean toward gloss and depth. Others lean toward slickness and easy washing, or toward toughness.
  • Rated life. Each maker rates its own coating. Lumen's paint coatings run from maker-rated up to 24 months to maker-rated up to 50 months to five years, properly applied and maintained. Real life depends on how the car is driven, washed and stored.
  • The finish they're made for. Matte paint, vinyl wraps and paint protection film need coatings made for them, with no added shine and no polishing first.

That is why I don't stick to one maker. One bottle can't be the right answer for every vehicle that pulls into a driveway. The rest of this note explains what's behind each of those differences, so the choice makes sense on its own terms.

The chemistry, in plain terms

Makers rarely publish full formulas, so I won't pretend to know what is in every bottle. The published science does describe the main families, and it explains why two coatings that both say "ceramic" can behave differently.

Polysilazane: a polymer that turns into glass

Polysilazane is a polymer chain of alternating silicon and nitrogen atoms. As it cures, it reacts with moisture and oxygen in the air and converts to silica (SiO2). One coating chemist's technical note shows the change in infrared spectra: the silicon-nitrogen signal falls while the silicon-oxygen signal rises.1

–(Si–N)n– → –(Si–O)n–

A university study of thin polysilazane films shows how far that conversion goes at room temperature. Films about 0.2 µm thick, exposed to ammonia vapor at room temperature, dropped from 33.6 to 0.5 atomic percent nitrogen, and their refractive index fell from 1.56 to 1.48. Only after firing at 900 °C did the index reach 1.45, close to dense silica glass. The authors concluded the room-temperature films "are not identical to pure and dense silica glass."2

Two practical points follow. A coating cured on a car, at air temperature, is a glass-like network, not a pane of glass. And cure is a chemical process that takes time, which is why makers give cure windows: one keeps its coating out of direct sun and away from water for the first 12 hours,11 and another lists 1 to 2 hours to dry and 3 to 5 days to full cure at 25 °C.10

Siloxane and sol-gel silica

The other common route starts from silicon alkoxides, molecules with a silicon atom wrapped in small organic groups. In the sol-gel process they react with water (hydrolysis) and then link to each other (condensation), building an oxide network at temperatures far below those used to make conventional glass.4 A review of sol-gel silica coatings notes that this can happen at ambient temperature, which is what makes the chemistry usable on a car.3

Purely inorganic silica films have limits. The same review describes them as typically nanometers to under a micron thick and prone to cracking, and it says adding organic components improves crack resistance and allows thicker layers, while the inorganic part contributes hardness and abrasion resistance.3 That trade-off, glass for toughness and organic groups for flexibility, is the design space most car coatings live in.

Why water beads: the surface groups and fluorinated toppers

Here is a detail that surprises people: clean silica is not very water-repellent. In the study above, the converted film's water contact angle fell from 94° to 67°, and after firing to a near-pure silica film it was 29°.2 Chemists call anything under 90° wetting.6 My read of those numbers: the tall beads you see on a coated car come mostly from what sits at the surface of the network: methyl groups bonded to silicon (one maker's spectra show them still present after cure),1 and, in many products, fluorinated groups.

Fluorine lowers the surface energy further. The same technical note shows that adding fluorine compounds at a surface lowers its critical surface tension and raises the contact angle, and it describes silanes carrying a perfluoroalkyl group that bond to glass and leave the fluorinated tail facing out.1 A review of water-repellent surfaces ranks CF3 groups lowest in surface energy among common groups, and reports about 120° on a flat surface of tightly packed CF3 groups. Going much higher than that needs surface roughness, not just chemistry.5

This is what top layers and toppers are for. The base does the structural work; a thin, low-energy layer on top does the beading and slickness, and it is the part that wears first. More on that wear, and how to bring beading back, is in Why ceramic coatings stop beading.

Layer systems

Coatings are thin. One maker specifies up to 2 µm per layer and describes its coating as multi-layer.10 Factory clear coat is usually 35–50 µm,15 so even several coating layers are a small fraction of it. Systems come in two shapes:

  • One layer. One maker describes its single-layer coating as "designed as a one-layer coating," maker-rated up to 36 months.11 Lumen's Essential and Signature are single layers.
  • Base plus top. Another product from that maker is a two-stage system: a base layer for "strength and resistance," then a top coat that "enhances surface slickness and prolongs the durability of the hydrophobic effect," maker-rated up to 50 months.12 Lumen's Reserve works the same way: a base layer for strength and chemical resistance, a top layer for beading, slickness and dirt release.

What "9H" means, and what it doesn't

Coating labels love "9H." It comes from the pencil hardness test. Pencil leads graded from 9B (softest) through HB and F to 9H (hardest) are moved across the surface in a defined way, and the rating is the hardest grade that doesn't damage it.8 The standard method, ASTM D3363, describes itself as a "rapid, inexpensive" way to rate the film hardness of an organic coating on a metal or similarly hard substrate.7

The standard is also candid about its limits. It says the method is useful for "relative rankings for a series of coatings," that caution is needed when comparing coatings of similar hardness, that results can vary between laboratories, and that pencils should come from the same maker and batch.7 Three more limits matter for car coatings:

  • Thickness. Coating researchers testing hardness note that ASTM methods call for a layer of at least 25 µm to keep the substrate from influencing the result.4 A car coating at up to 2 µm per layer10 is far thinner than that, so a pencil result on a coated panel is partly a measurement of what's underneath.
  • Hardness isn't scratch resistance. In one study of silica hybrid coatings, pencil and pendulum hardness barely changed as silica content rose, yet scratch resistance improved, which the authors traced to a smoother surface.4 NIST researchers tie clear coat scratch resistance to crosslink density and test for deformation, fracture and resilience, not a single hardness number.9
  • It's a different load. Wash marring is a few micrometers deep and most often comes from car washing.9 A rock at highway speed is a different event entirely, which is why chips need film.

So "9H" is a real test result, but it doesn't tell you how a coating will hold up to your wash routine or how many years it will last. I don't choose coatings by it.

Chemical resistance and pH

Chemical resistance is where coatings differ in ways you'll actually notice in Utah. Two maker specs are worth knowing:

  • The single-layer coating above and the two-stage system both list a pH tolerance of 2 to 11.11,12 That range is what makes a mild acidic mineral remover, or an alkaline pre-wash, safe on those coatings. Outside the range, the maker isn't standing behind it.
  • Both makers say to wash with a pH-neutral shampoo and avoid harsh chemicals.11,12

Why it matters: beads of water that dry on paint can pick up airborne pollutants and leave more strongly acidic residue behind, and one coating chemist's stain test used dirt suspended in water acidified with sulfuric acid.1 Winter adds salt and mag chloride brine, which is covered in Mag chloride, road salt and your car. Hard-water minerals are the other big one: Hard-water spots in Utah explains why an acidic decon is the fix, and why the coating's pH rating decides whether it's safe.

Slickness: contact angle vs. sliding angle

A tall bead and a slick surface aren't the same thing. The static contact angle says how round a drop sits. What owners notice is whether drops roll off. That depends on contact angle hysteresis, the gap between a drop's advancing angle (its front edge) and receding angle (its back edge):6

Δθ = θA − θR

The smaller that gap, the less a drop clings, and the lower the tilt it needs to slide. Surface scientists call a surface superhydrophobic when its contact angle is above 150° and its sliding angle is under 10°.5 Car coatings aren't in that class on flat paint: one maker lists a water contact angle of 110° for its product,10 in line with the roughly 120° ceiling for a flat fluorinated surface.5

Higher beading isn't automatically better, either. One coating chemist argues that tall beads which sit and dry leave water spots, and describes a coating deliberately tuned to 75–80° so less water stays behind.1 For a car that lives under sprinklers, a coating that sheets and sheds water can be a better match than one that makes perfect, stationary beads. The friction side of slickness, and why it reduces wash marring, is in How to wash a ceramic-coated car.

Gloss and refractive index

Gloss is the surface's ability to reflect more light close to the mirror direction than in others. The standard method, ASTM D523, measures it at 20°, 60° or 85° and says the readings correlate with what people see as shininess.13 How much light a clear surface reflects head-on depends on its refractive index, n:14

R = (n − 1)2 / (n + 1)2

An illustration, using the indexes from the polysilazane study above:2 a room-temperature film at n = 1.48 reflects about 3.7% of light at normal incidence; a fired, near-dense silica film at n = 1.45 reflects about 3.4%; the uncured film at n = 1.56 about 4.8%. Those are differences of about a percentage point. My read of that arithmetic: chemistry alone can't make a big difference in how much light the surface reflects. What changes the look is where the reflected light goes. A flat, defect-free surface sends it back in the mirror direction; swirls and haze scatter it. That's why most of a show car's gloss comes from correction, and why a coating over swirls just preserves the swirls.

What a gloss-focused coating can do is keep that corrected surface looking that way longer. In one maker's accelerated weathering test, coated panels lost 60° gloss nearly 25% more slowly than uncoated ones.1

The four questions I ask

  1. Where is it parked? A garage, a driveway under sprinklers, or a lot in full sun.
  2. How is it washed? By hand at home, at an automatic wash, or rarely.
  3. What does it go through? Winter salt and mag chloride, trails and brush, job-site dust.
  4. How long will you keep it? A lease ending next year and a truck you'll drive for a decade call for different coatings.

I rinse a panel at the walkaround to see how the paint sheds water today, inspect it under lights, and measure paint depth before any polishing.15 Why that measurement comes first is in Paint depth gauging.

How parking, washing and exposure change the choice

  • Garage-kept. Less sun, fewer mineral spots, less fallout. The coating's job is mostly to keep corrected paint looking corrected, so a gloss-focused single layer fits.
  • Outside, under sprinklers. Minerals land on the coating all season. A coating that sheds water, plus a maker pH rating that allows an acidic decon, matters more than peak beading.1,11 Pointing the sprinklers away from the car does more than any product.
  • Hand-washed at home. Any of the levels work, and the slick top of a two-layer system makes each wash easier.
  • Automatic brush washes. Wash marring is mostly a washing problem,9 and makers' care instructions call for pH-neutral shampoo and no harsh chemicals.11 If brushes are the plan, it's worth talking through before buying a long-rated coating.
  • Salt, trails and job sites. Chemical resistance and dirt release lead, which points to a two-layer system.

Rated life vs. real life

Every durability number on this site is the maker's own rating for its own product, and makers frame them carefully. One rates its coatings in both time and distance: up to 36 months or 40,000 km for the single layer, up to 50 months or 50,000 km for the two-stage system.11,12 Read those as "up to," under the maker's assumed care.

Real life depends on how the car is driven, washed and stored. A garage-kept weekend car and a daily commuter that sits under sprinklers can wear the same coating very differently. Often what fades first is the low-energy top surface, not the whole coating, and a decon and topper brings it back. To be straight about the limits: I didn't find published data that puts a number on how much Utah sun, altitude or salt shortens a given coating's life, so I won't guess one.

The maintenance commitment

A coating asks for a routine. Before choosing a longer-rated level, make sure it fits yours:

  • The first days. Keep it dry and out of the weather for the cure window its maker gives, from 12 hours to several days depending on the product.10,11
  • Every wash. pH-neutral shampoo, no harsh chemicals,11,12 no automatic brush washes, and dry the car rather than letting it air dry.
  • Now and then. A topper or the maker's booster to refresh the slick top surface,12 and a decon within the coating's pH range when minerals mute the beading.
  • Once a year. On coatings I install, the Annual Coating Service: inspection, a decon wash, iron removal and a booster top-up. The warranty depends on it.

If that sounds like more than you want, the lower rungs exist for a reason: a ceramic sealant, maker-rated up to 6 months or 50 washes, or the Halo spray coating, maker-rated up to 12–18 months, renewed at a detail. How they compare is in Ceramic coating, ceramic sealant, wax or PPF.

What a coating won't do

  • It won't stop rock chips or deep scratches. That's what paint protection film is for.
  • It won't hide swirls. A coating locks in whatever is under it, so the paint is polished first.1
  • It doesn't mean you never wash again. It makes washing easier, and it still needs the right care to reach its rated life.
  • It won't stop water spots on its own. Beads that dry in place still leave their minerals.1

Questions

Does a more expensive coating always last longer?

Not always. Rated life, layers and what the coating is tuned for all change the price. A gloss-focused coating can cost more than a tougher one with a similar rating. That's why I pick by how you use the vehicle, not by price alone.

Can you put a coating over swirls?

You can, but the coating locks the swirls in. The paint is always polished first, and paint depth is measured on every panel so only what's needed comes off.

Where do you apply it?

At your place. In your garage when you have one, so the coating cures out of the sun, wind and dust. No garage? I use coatings made for outdoor application and plan the day around the weather. I tell you the cure times before I hand back the keys.

Is a 9H coating harder than a 7H one?

On a pencil hardness test, under the same conditions, yes. But the standard itself says the test gives relative rankings and that results vary between labs, and a coating a couple of microns thick is too thin to be tested apart from the paint under it. It doesn't predict scratch resistance in washing or how long the coating lasts.

Is a two-layer coating worth it?

For a car you'll keep for years, or one that sees salt, trails or job sites, it usually fits: the base carries the chemical resistance and the top layer carries the slickness. For a lease or a car you'll sell in two years, a single layer is often all it needs.

What does a coating's pH rating mean for me?

It tells you which cleaners the maker stands behind. A coating rated for pH 2 to 11 can take a mild acidic mineral remover and an alkaline pre-wash. Day to day, a pH-neutral shampoo is still the rule.
